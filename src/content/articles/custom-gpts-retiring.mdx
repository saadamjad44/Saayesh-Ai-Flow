    answer: Four things, and all four have bitten people. Your previous conversations with the GPT do not come across. Custom actions are not part of the built-in migration and have to be rebuilt by hand. Sharing permissions do not transfer, so the new plugin starts private even if the GPT was shared with your whole team. And the model you originally picked for the GPT is not carried over either.
  - question: Will my shared GPT links keep working?
    answer: No. After the retirement date the GPT pages themselves become inaccessible, which means any link you have handed to a client, put in an onboarding document or pinned in a team chat stops working.     answer: For most small businesses a Project is enough, and it is the simpler answer. Build a plugin when other people have to use the same thing, when it has to connect to outside apps, or when you want it callable from any chat rather than only inside one workspace.
  - question: What if I only use someone else's custom GPT and did not build it?
    answer: You cannot migrate it — only its creator can.     answer: A simple one is minutes. Open My GPTs, choose the GPT, pick the migrate option when it appears, then test the result on the prompts you actually use it for before you rely on it. Budget an afternoon if your GPT had actions and an audience, and ten minutes if it did not.

**OpenAI is retiring custom GPTs. Existing ones stop working on 11 December 2026, and you can no longer create new ones — that door closed on 26 October 2026.** If your business runs on a custom GPT, you have weeks to move it, and the move is not a single button for everything it held.

This is the practical version of that job: the dates, what a plugin is, exactly what carries across and what you rebuild by hand, and the decision most coverage skips — whether you need a plugin at all, because for a lot of small businesses a Project is the simpler and better answer.

The second row is the one people misread. It has already happened. If you have been meaning to build a custom GPT for your business, that option is gone and nothing on this page brings it back — what follows is about rescuing the ones that exist and choosing where new work goes instead.

## What a plugin actually is

OpenAI's direct successor to the custom GPT is a **plugin**: a package that bundles reusable instructions, reference material and connections to other apps.

The difference is not in what it holds. It is in how you reach it. A custom GPT was a destination — you opened it, and you were in a conversation with that persona. A plugin is a tool you call from inside an ordinary chat. The capability survives; the "go somewhere to use it" shape does not.

That has one genuinely good consequence and one annoyance. The good one: a plugin's instructions are available to you in the chat you are already in, rather than requiring you to leave and start again somewhere else. The annoyance: a plugin starts private and has to be shared and installed deliberately, so the casual "here's a link, just use it" distribution that made GPTs spread does not come across with it.

OpenAI has also split the rest of what a custom GPT used to do across features that already existed:

- **Skills** — a reusable set of instructions for a process you repeat.
- **Projects** — a workspace that keeps chats, files and standing instructions together.
- **Apps** — the connections out to services like your mail, storage or CRM.
- **ChatGPT Work and workspace agents** — where OpenAI points action-heavy, multi-system GPTs inside a company workspace.

This is why "what replaces custom GPTs" has no single answer. The honest answer is that a custom GPT was several things wearing one coat, and you now pick the piece you were actually using.

Two of those deserve emphasis, because they are the ones that turn a ten-minute job into a bad week.

**Custom actions.** If your GPT called an API — pulled stock levels, looked up a booking, posted to a system of yours — none of that comes across. It has to be rebuilt, and if a developer or agency set it up for you originally, they need to be in the room. Find out now whether that is your situation, not in the first week of December.

**Sharing.** A GPT shared with your team becomes a private plugin. Nobody is told. The failure mode is quiet: you migrate, you test it yourself, it works perfectly, and three colleagues go on using the old GPT until the day it vanishes.

<Callout tone="tip" heading="Save the conversations first">
  Conversation history does not migrate, and GPT pages go away. If any of those threads contain work
  you would miss — a worked-out process, a decision and its reasoning, drafts you still refer to —
  copy them out now. This is the only part of the job that cannot be done late.
</Callout>

## Most small businesses do not need a plugin

Here is the part the migration guides do not say, because they are written for IT departments moving dozens of GPTs for a whole organisation.

Think about what your custom GPT really was. For the large majority of small businesses, it was a set of instructions — "you are our brand voice, here is how we write, never say these words" — plus two or three reference files, used by exactly one person. No actions. No API. No audience.

If that is yours, **a Project is the better destination.** It holds standing instructions and files in one place, it keeps the related chats with them, there is nothing to publish and nobody has to install anything. You are not migrating a product; you are putting your instructions somewhere they stay.

The useful question is not "how do I migrate my GPT" but "which one thing was my GPT doing for me". Answer that and the destination is obvious, and in about half of cases it is less work than the migration path.

## How to migrate one

<Steps>
  <Step title="Copy out anything in the conversations">
    History does not come across and the GPT pages disappear. Save the threads that contain work you
    would miss. Do this first — it is the only step with no second chance.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide where it should actually go">
    Use the table above. If it was yours alone and had no app connections, build a Project and stop;
    you do not need the migration flow at all.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Open My GPTs and start the migration">
    Select the GPT and choose the option to migrate it to a plugin, where that is offered on your
    account. Instructions, knowledge files and connected apps come across in one go. The original
    becomes read-only but stays reachable until the retirement date, so you keep a reference copy.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rebuild custom actions, if it had any">
    These are not in the migration. Involve whoever configured them, and expect a security or
    permissions review if the actions touch anything of yours that matters.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test it on the prompts you really use">
    Not one friendly prompt — the three or four requests this thing exists for, including the
    awkward one. The model choice did not transfer, so output may genuinely read differently.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set sharing, then tell the humans">
    The plugin starts private. Share it, have your users install it, and send a line telling them
    the old link stops working in December. Replace the link anywhere it is written down —
    onboarding docs, pinned chat messages, your own notes.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## If you only use someone else's GPT

You cannot migrate it. Only the creator can, and nothing you do in your own account changes that.

Two ways out, and the second is usually faster than people expect:

- **Ask the creator** whether a plugin replacement is coming, and get it installed before the date rather than after. If it is a GPT from the store with no named owner behind it, assume the answer is no.
- **Rebuild the part you rely on.** You almost certainly depend on the instructions rather than anything technical — the way it formats things, the questions it asks you, the tone it holds. Put that in a Project of your own and you own it permanently, which is the better position anyway.

That second route has a wider lesson in it. A workflow that lives inside somebody else's GPT in somebody else's store was always one product decision away from ending, and this is that decision arriving. Where a process matters to how your business runs, keep the instructions somewhere you control.
