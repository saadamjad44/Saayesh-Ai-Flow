  - question: What is AI customer service automation?
    answer: It is software that handles part of your customer support without you - reading an incoming question, finding the answer in your own documentation, and either replying, drafting a reply for you to approve, or passing it to a person. The AI part is the reading and the writing. The automation part is everything around it, such as what triggers it, where the answer comes from, who gets notified, and what is recorded afterwards.
  - question: How does AI decide whether it can answer a question?
    answer: Most systems run the same five stages. They interpret the question to work out what is being asked, search your connected content for passages that match, draft an answer grounded in what they found, score their own confidence in that answer, and then act on the score - reply, draft for review, or escalate. The confidence threshold is usually something you control, and it is the single most important setting in the whole system.
  - question: What is ticket deflection?
    answer: It is the support industry's term for a question being answered before it becomes a ticket a person has to handle, usually through self-service content or an automated reply. It is worth knowing the word because vendors sell on it, and worth treating carefully as a measure - a question that was answered badly and simply abandoned also counts as deflected in most reporting, which is why deflection on its own is a misleading success metric.
  - question: What should I automate first in customer support?
    answer: The questions you answer most often that have a single correct answer written down somewhere - delivery times, opening hours, what is included, how a process works. High volume, no judgement, and instantly checkable. Automate those, measure for a month, and only then consider anything requiring judgement. Starting with your hardest tickets is the most common mistake and the fastest route to abandoning the project.

Most pages on this subject sell an outcome: automate most of your support, deflect most of your tickets, free up your team. This one explains the machinery instead, because once you can see the five things happening behind a chat window, you can tell which of the claims are plausible for your business and which are not.

It is also the more useful way round. The settings that decide whether this works for you are all inside that machinery, and most of them are yours to configure.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Five stages run behind every automated reply:** interpret, search, draft, score confidence, then act on the score.
- **The confidence threshold is the most important setting you own.** It decides what gets answered, drafted, or escalated.
- **Your documentation is the limiting factor,** not the model. The search stage can only find what you have written.
- **Draft before send,** and switch categories to automatic one at a time once they have earned it.
- **Deflection rate is a misleading measure on its own** — an abandoned conversation counts as deflected. Read transcripts weekly.

</KeyTakeaways>

## The five stages behind an automated reply

Whatever the product is called, this is the sequence. Knowing it is what lets you diagnose a bad answer rather than just be disappointed by it.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Interpret the question">
    The model reads what the customer wrote and works out what is being asked, independently of
    wording. This is the stage that genuinely works well and the reason rule-based keyword matching
    was replaced: "has it shipped yet", "where's my stuff" and "tracking?" all resolve to the same
    intent without you writing a rule for each.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Search your content">
    The system looks through whatever you have connected — help centre, FAQ pages, uploaded
    documents, sometimes past tickets — for the passages that answer it. **This stage sets your
    ceiling.** It cannot find what you have not written, and if it finds two passages that disagree
    it has no way to know which is current.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Draft an answer">
    The model writes a reply based on the passages it retrieved, rather than from general knowledge.
    Grounding the answer in retrieved content is what keeps it specific to your business; it is also
    why a thin help centre produces vague replies rather than wrong ones, which is the better of the
    two failure modes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Score its own confidence">
    The system rates how well the retrieved content actually answered the question. This number is
    usually exposed to you as a threshold, and it is the main dial you own: set it low and more
    questions get answered, including some that should not have been; set it high and more reach
    you, which costs time but almost never costs trust.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Act on the score">
    Above the threshold it replies or drafts. Below it, it should say it does not know and escalate.
    What it does at that boundary is the behaviour to test before you trust any of it — ask
    something your documentation genuinely does not cover and watch what happens.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Two consequences fall out of this that vendor pages rarely state plainly.

First, **the quality of your documentation matters more than the choice of product**, because stage two is where answers come from. Businesses that are disappointed by this software have almost always bought it instead of writing their answers rather than after.

Second, **the characteristic failure is a confident wrong answer**, produced when stage two retrieves something that looks relevant but is out of date or applies to a different case. Nothing in the pipeline flags that. Only a person reading transcripts catches it, which is why that is a permanent task rather than a setup step.

## Designing the escalation

This is the part that is almost always under-designed, and it matters more than the answering.

**Make the route to a person visible and single-click.** Hidden handover is the thing customers genuinely resent, far more than a bot they can escape.

**Pass the context across.** When it hands over, you should receive the full conversation, not a fresh start. Asking a customer to repeat themselves after they have already explained it is worse than not having automated anything.

**Decide what happens out of hours.** An escalation at 2am that nobody sees until 9am needs to tell the customer that, honestly. A promise of "someone will be right with you" at 2am is a lie your software told on your behalf.

**Define the categories that always escalate,** by rule. See the bottom of the table above.

**Give it a real "I don't know".** Explicitly instructed, and tested. The default behaviour of a language model is to produce a plausible answer rather than to stop, so this has to be configured rather than assumed.

<Callout tone="warning" heading="Draft mode is not a training-wheels phase">
  Draft mode — the system prepares, you release — is where this should start and, for anything
  involving judgement, where it should stay. It costs seconds per message and gives you a complete
  view of the failures. Move one category at a time to automatic sending once it has run cleanly for
  a few weeks, and keep anything touching money, dates or policy in draft permanently.
</Callout>

## The numbers that actually tell you something

Vendors lead on **deflection rate** — the share of questions resolved without a person. Treat it with suspicion, for one specific reason: in most reporting, a customer who got a bad answer and gave up counts as deflected. The metric cannot tell success from abandonment, so on its own it is not evidence of anything.

Better measures, none of which require special tooling:

- **Escalation rate, and its trend.** A rising rate usually means your documentation has gone stale, not that the software got worse.
- **Repeat contacts.** The same person asking the same thing again is the clearest signal of a bad answer.
- **Human time per conversation** on the ones you still handle — including the time spent reading drafts and fixing them.
- **A weekly sample of transcripts, read by a person.** Ten is enough. This is the only measure that catches a confident wrong answer, and it is not optional.
- **Resolution time on what you did not automate,** which is the real benefit if this works: your attention goes to the harder cases.

## Where to start this week

Write down your ten most common customer questions and the correct answer to each. Publish them. Connect that content to whichever front end matches how enquiries actually reach you — chat for web traffic, inbox automation for email. Set the confidence threshold high, keep everything in draft, route price, refund and complaint questions straight to yourself by rule, and read every transcript for the first fortnight.

That sequence costs almost nothing, and it tells you within two weeks whether the rest is worth buying. It also leaves you better off even if you conclude it is not, because the answers are now written down — which was most of the work all along.
