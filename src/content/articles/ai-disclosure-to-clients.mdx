faq:
  - question: Do I legally have to tell clients I use AI?
    answer: In most ordinary client work, no — there is no general legal duty to announce that you used an AI tool to help produce something. The duties that do exist are narrower than the internet suggests and they attach to specific situations rather than to AI use in general. What binds most freelancers and agencies is not a statute at all. It is the contract they already signed, which may well restrict what they can put into a third-party tool even though it says nothing about AI.
  - question: Does the EU AI Act require me to disclose AI-generated content?
    answer: Only in a narrow set of cases, and probably not the one you are in. The marking obligations in Article 50 fall mainly on providers — the companies that build the AI systems — rather than on you for using one. The duty that applies to users of a system covers things like deepfakes and AI-generated text published on matters of public interest without human editorial review. Client marketing copy that you wrote with AI help and then edited yourself is not that. Article 50's obligations have applied since 2 August 2026, with a grace period running to 2 December 2026 for generative systems already on the market before that date.
  - question: What is the difference between disclosing that you used AI and disclosing what you put into it?
    answer: They are two separate questions and only one of them is usually binding. The first is about credit — whether a tool helped you produce the work. The second is about data — whether you sent your client's information to a third party. The first is mostly an ethics and expectations question. The second is frequently governed by the confidentiality and data clauses in your existing contract, which means it can be a breach whether or not anybody ever asks about AI.
  - question: Will telling a client I use AI cost me the work?
    answer: It can, with a client who equates AI with corners being cut. Being found out later is the worse outcome by a distance, because it reframes everything else you have delivered. The framing that holds up is not a confession that you used a tool — it is a statement that a named, accountable human checked the output and stands behind it. Most buyers are not objecting to the tool; they are checking that somebody is still responsible for the result.
  - question: How should I word an AI disclosure?
    answer: Put one or two plain sentences in the proposal rather than in the small print, say what the tool does and what you do, and name who is accountable. A workable form is to state that you use AI assistance for drafting and research, that every deliverable is reviewed and edited by you before it is sent, that you remain responsible for accuracy, and that you will not put their confidential material into any third-party tool without asking first. It fits in a proposal and answers the three things a client actually wants to know.
  - question: What should I do if a client asks me directly whether I used AI?
    answer: Answer honestly, in specific terms, and do it in the same message. Say which parts the tool touched, what you did to the output, and what you checked. A vague denial is the single most expensive move available to you here, because it converts a question about process into a question about your honesty, and the second one is not recoverable.
  - question: Can a client ban me from using AI on their work?
    answer: Yes, and some do, usually through a clause about third-party tools and confidential information rather than a clause naming AI. If the restriction is in the contract, it binds you regardless of what you think of it. If you cannot deliver profitably without AI assistance, that is a conversation to have before signing rather than a rule to quietly work around — and the quiet route is also the one most likely to end in a dispute you lose.
  - question: Do I need a written AI policy for my business?
    answer: If anyone other than you does client work, yes, and it can be one page. Name the tools people may use and on which accounts, sort your data into what may and may not go into them, require that a human reviews anything before it leaves the business, and name one person to ask about the grey cases. The value is not the document; it is that you can answer a client's question in one consistent sentence rather than guessing on the spot.

**For most client work there is no general legal duty to announce that AI helped you produce something. The thing that does bind you is usually your own contract — and it rarely mentions AI at all.**

This question gets answered badly in both directions. One half of the internet tells freelancers the EU AI Act now forces them to label everything; the other half says disclosure is purely optional and a bad idea commercially. Both are reading one narrow rule as if it were a general one, and both miss the obligation that actually catches people.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **There are two different disclosures,** and only one is commonly binding: _we used a tool_ and _we sent your information to a third party_.
- **No general legal duty** attaches to using AI to help produce ordinary client work.
- **The EU AI Act's Article 50 is narrower than the summaries suggest** — most of its marking duties fall on the companies building AI systems, not on you for using one.
- **Your confidentiality and data clauses are the real constraint,** and they bind you even though they never say "AI".
- **Being found out later is much worse than disclosing,** because it turns a process question into an honesty question.
- **The framing that works names a responsible human,** not the tool.
- **If anyone else does client work for you, write the one-page rule down** — otherwise your answer to a client changes depending on who they ask.

</KeyTakeaways>

Almost every argument about this collapses once you separate the two questions hiding inside it.

The first row is the one everybody debates. The second is the one that creates actual liability, and it does so whether or not anyone ever raises the subject of AI, because the clause does not need to mention AI to cover it. "You will not disclose confidential information to third parties without written consent" is a sentence that has been in contracts for decades, and pasting a client's customer list into an AI tool is disclosing it to a third party.

That is the reframe worth carrying away. You may well have no obligation to tell a client that AI helped you write their landing page, and simultaneously have a binding obligation not to have put their unpublished financials into the tool that helped you.

## What the law actually requires

### The EU AI Act, read properly

Article 50's transparency obligations have applied since **2 August 2026**, and they are the source of nearly every overstated claim on this topic. What they say, in the parts that could touch a small business:

- **Providers** — the companies that build AI systems — must mark synthetic audio, image, video and text so it is machine-readable and detectable as AI-generated. That duty is OpenAI's and Anthropic's, not yours.
- **Providers** of systems that interact directly with people must make sure people know they are talking to an AI, unless that is already obvious.
- **Deployers** — which can include you — must disclose deepfakes, and must disclose AI-generated or manipulated **text published on matters of public interest** where it has not had human editorial review with a person taking responsibility for publishing it.
- **Deployers** must also tell people when they are using emotion recognition or biometric categorisation on them.

A grace period runs to **2 December 2026** for the machine-readable marking of generative systems already on the market before August, and content generated before that date does not have to be labelled retroactively. The Commission issued non-binding guidance on Article 50 in July 2026, and the EU AI Office published a voluntary code of practice on transparency of AI-generated content at the end of that month. Penalties at the top of the scale reach €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover.

Now read the third bullet against what you probably do. A freelancer who drafts a client's marketing email with AI help, edits it, and sends it for the client's approval is not publishing unreviewed text on a matter of public interest. There is human editorial review, there is a person responsible, and it is not public-interest commentary. The duty that people quote at freelancers mostly does not reach them.

<Callout tone="note" heading="Where it does reach you">
  Three situations change the answer: you publish AI-generated commentary on matters of public
  interest without a human reviewing it, you produce synthetic audio or video of a real person, or
  you run emotion recognition or biometric categorisation on people. If any of those describe your
  work, this page is not enough and the obligations are specific.
</Callout>

### Outside the EU

There is no equivalent across-the-board duty in most jurisdictions to say that AI helped you produce a piece of work. What exists instead is a patchwork that is easy to miss:

- **Regulated professions** — legal, accounting, medical, financial advice — increasingly have rules or guidance from their own bodies, and those can be stricter than any statute.
- **Platforms and marketplaces** sometimes have their own terms about AI-produced deliverables.
- **Public sector and enterprise procurement** routinely asks the question directly now, in the questionnaire rather than the contract.
- **Consumer protection law** already covers misleading claims, which is the route by which "written by our expert team" becomes a problem if it was not.

## Your contract is the part that binds you

Before worrying about legislation, read the agreement you have actually signed. Four clause types do the work, and none of them will contain the word "AI":

- **Confidentiality.** Restricts disclosing the client's information to third parties. An AI tool is a third party.
- **Data processing.** May require you to name your sub-processors, keep data in certain regions, or get consent before adding a new one.
- **Warranties about the work.** Promises of originality, accuracy, or that the deliverable does not infringe anyone's rights. AI output can put pressure on all three, and the promise is yours regardless of how the draft was produced.
- **Subcontracting.** Sometimes requires consent before anyone other than you works on the deliverable. Whether a tool counts is arguable; the argument is one you would rather not be having.

## How to word it

Disclosure fails when it reads like a confession and when it arrives late. Put it in the proposal, in plain sentences, before anyone has paid you anything.

What a client is actually asking, underneath the question, is three things: did a competent person check this, who is accountable if it is wrong, and where did my information go. Answer those and the tool becomes uninteresting.

A form that holds up in a proposal:

> I use AI assistance for research and first drafts. Every deliverable is reviewed and edited by me before it reaches you, and I am responsible for its accuracy. I will not put your confidential material into any third-party tool without asking you first.

Three sentences, and each is doing a job. The first is honest about the process without overstating or understating it. The second names a responsible human — the part that reassures. The third answers the data question before it is asked, which is the one that could otherwise become a contractual problem.

If you want a version for a client who has asked after the fact, be more specific rather than more general: name which parts of the work the tool touched, what you did to the output, and what you verified. Specificity reads as control. Vagueness reads as evasion, even when it is not.

## The one thing not to do

Do not deny it.

A client who asks whether you used AI has usually already decided the answer and is checking what you will say. The cost of a vague or false denial is not that they learn you used a tool. It is that they stop trusting the rest of the work, including the parts you did entirely yourself, and there is no version of the conversation afterwards that recovers that.

This is the whole commercial case for putting the line in the proposal. It is not that disclosure is free — with some clients it genuinely is not. It is that disclosure is cheap and discovery is not, and you only control which of the two happens while you are still the one choosing.

## Write down your own rule

If anyone besides you touches client work, your answer to this question has to be the same whoever gets asked. One page is enough, and the useful version names only five things:

- **Which tools** people may use, and on which accounts — a company workspace rather than personal logins, so the setting is yours to set. See [ChatGPT Business vs Plus](/ai-business-tools/chatgpt-business-vs-plus/) for why that distinction matters more than it looks.
- **Which data may go in,** and which never may. Write the second list first; it is shorter and it is the one that matters.
- **Who reviews output** before it leaves the business, and that it is always a named person.
- **What you tell clients,** in the exact words, so nobody has to improvise.
- **Who decides the grey cases.** One name.

That document is also the thing you hand over when a client, insurer or tender asks how you handle AI — a question that is now routine and that nobody answers well off the cuff.
