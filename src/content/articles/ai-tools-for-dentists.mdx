That is not useless information, but it is a sales tool rather than a buying tool, and it is the reason the same five products keep appearing in different orders depending on who is paying.

This guide takes the other approach. It names the categories that genuinely exist, the questions that separate a good fit from an expensive mistake, and the compliance details that most ranked lists wave past in a sentence. The goal is not to tell you which product to buy. It is to leave you able to run that conversation yourself, with whichever vendor is in front of you.

Four categories of AI tool are sold to dental practices today, and they are not really competitors - they do different jobs, run against different parts of the practice, and should be bought in a sensible order rather than together.

First, the first row is where most small and mid-size practices should start, because the task is narrow enough to measure, the customer is not yet in your chair, and the failure mode is visible - a bad message from the AI gets corrected before the patient arrives. ## The questions that actually separate products

Once you have picked the category, these are what distinguish one product from another. Ask them in this order - the early ones disqualify faster.

### 1. Will you sign a Business Associate Agreement, and under what data terms?

This is the first question because it decides whether the tool is legally usable with patient information at all. In the United States, a HIPAA-covered dental practice may only share Protected Health Information with a vendor that has signed a BAA with you. "HIPAA compliant" on a marketing page is not a BAA. "HIPAA ready" is definitely not a BAA. Ask for the agreement by name, read the data-use terms attached to it, and note three things in particular:

- **Training.** Is your practice's data used to train the vendor's models, in any form, including de-identified?
- **Retention.** How long is a recording, transcript, or message kept, and can you force deletion?
- **Access.** Who at the vendor can see the data, under what controls, and is that logged?

A vendor who hesitates on any of these is answering the question. If you are outside the US, substitute your equivalent regime - the UK's UK GDPR plus the NHS data protection toolkit, the EU's GDPR with any national health-data rules, and so on - and ask the same three questions.

### 2. How are you billed, and what counts as a billable unit?

This is the hardest thing to undo after a signature, because the pricing model decides what a busy month costs far more than the published rate does. Dental AI tools are commonly billed per seat, per patient, per transcribed minute, per message or call handled, per image read, or against a pool of credits that different operations consume at different rates.

So ask, specifically:

- What is one billable unit, in plain words?
- How are failed runs counted? A scribe that mis-transcribes an appointment and has to be reviewed from scratch is a different cost than one that works.
- How are escalated or handed-over conversations counted? A front-desk AI that transfers a third of calls to your receptionist may or may not charge you for them, and the answer changes the economics completely.
- Is there a cap, an alert, or an overage rate?

Then price your **peak** month at the top of your expected range. A front-desk AI costs most in the week your receptionist is off sick, which is the week you are least likely to be reviewing invoices.

### 3. Can it run in draft or approval mode?

Non-negotiable for anything that writes to a patient or into a chart. You want the AI preparing text your team releases, at least until it has earned more than that. Ask whether approval can be set per action - drafting a recall text alone, but asking before confirming an appointment - because all-or-nothing autonomy is far less useful than it sounds.

A scribe that cannot be held in draft mode is telling you what stage of the market it is at. So is a receptionist tool that defaults to sending without review.

### 4. What does it do when it does not know?

The single most informative question you can ask, and the one most likely to be answered vaguely. You are looking for an explicit, configurable behaviour: say it does not know, escalate, and stop. The failure mode you are guarding against is not silence - it is a confident, plausible, wrong answer, delivered to a patient in your practice's name.

Ask to see that behaviour in a trial, on a question the tool has not been trained on. Make up something your practice policy does not cover and watch what happens.

### 5. What can you see afterwards?

You need a log of what the tool did and why - which patient, which message, which source it used, which clinician approved it. Without that, debugging is guesswork, and a question from a regulator, an insurer, or a patient cannot be answered. For scribes this matters doubly, because the record of what the AI drafted and what the clinician changed is what makes the chart defensible.

### 6. Does it fit the practice management system you already run?

Open Dental, Dentrix, Eaglesoft, and CareCloud are the names that keep appearing in dental AI vendor documentation. An AI tool that forces a double-entry workflow - the AI writes somewhere, and then a human copies it into your actual system - is not a saving, it is extra work badly disguised. Ask to see the integration in a demo, not described in a slide.

### 7. How do you turn it off?

Mid-run, immediately, and without losing the record of what it had already done. Ask before you need it, so the answer is not improvised during an incident.

## What a sensible first purchase looks like

1. **Name the bottleneck.** Not the thing that annoys you most - the thing where time is measurably being lost. Phone calls missed? Charting time at the end of each day? Recall patients not booking? Pick one.
2. **Measure the baseline for two weeks.** You cannot grade an AI tool against a standard you have not established. Count missed calls, count after-hours charting minutes, count no-shows - whichever matches your bottleneck.
3. **Pick the category, then shortlist two products.** Two, not six. Beyond two you are comparing marketing pages rather than behaviour, and dental AI marketing is particularly polished.
4. **Ask both vendors the seven questions above.** In writing where possible. The answers vary more than the websites do.
5. **Trial both on the same real work**, with draft mode on and a clinician or office manager approving every output for the first two weeks.
6. **Count review time as cost.** A scribe that saves fifteen minutes of charting and adds ten minutes of review saved you five, not fifteen. [How much does AI cost for a small business](/ai-business-tools/how-much-does-ai-cost-small-business/) covers the wider arithmetic of what an AI stack actually comes to.
7. **Decide on evidence, then widen one setting at a time.** More autonomy, more patient types, or more hours of day covered - never two at once.

## Where general-purpose assistants fit in

A practice can get a real amount of mileage out of the general assistants it probably already has access to - [Claude](/ai-business-tools/claude-for-small-business/), ChatGPT, or the Copilot that comes with a Microsoft 365 business subscription - as long as the work is kept away from patient information.

The jobs that fit this shape, in rough order of safety:

- **Website copy, treatment explainers, FAQ pages** that go to the whole public and contain no patient data.
- **Marketing drafts** - social captions, newsletter sections, Google Business posts - cleared by a human before publication.
- **Staff training material** and internal SOPs, with clinical content reviewed by whoever actually owns the clinical standard.
- **Draft replies to non-patient enquiries** - suppliers, recruiters, representatives - where the content involves no PHI.
- **Summaries of CPD articles or policy updates** you need to read but do not have time to read in full.

The important discipline is a bright line between these tasks and anything containing PHI. ## When not to buy any of it

Four situations where the honest recommendation is to spend nothing on dental AI this quarter:

**Your practice management system already does the task.** Modern Dentrix, Eaglesoft, Open Dental, and CareCloud installations ship with scheduling automation, recall messages, and claim status features. Buying another tool to do what you already pay for is the easiest overspend in dentistry.

**You have not fixed the process yet.** Automating a broken recall workflow produces more broken recall at scale. Fix the manual version first, then automate the version that works.

**The task is clinical.** AI-assisted imaging, caries detection, and anything diagnostic belong on the clinical procurement track, with your associate dentists and your regulator's guidance, not alongside a receptionist tool.

**Nobody in the practice has time to own it.** AI tools without an owner drift into disuse within a quarter and stay on the invoice for a year. If no named person is going to review the outputs, read the logs, and raise issues with the vendor, do not buy the tool.

## The honest summary

Dental AI in 2026 is genuinely useful at the narrow, administrative end, and genuinely overclaimed at the broad, "autonomous practice" end. The categories that work today are the ones where the task is narrow enough to review, the patient has not yet entered the clinical workflow, and a person is approving the output.

Across every category, the shopping list is short: a signed BAA whose data terms you have read, billing you have modelled at peak, draft mode you can scope per action, an explicit do-not-know behaviour you have watched in a trial, a clean integration with the system you already use, and a log you can actually open. A product that gives you those six is a reasonable bet whatever position it holds on somebody else's ranked list.
