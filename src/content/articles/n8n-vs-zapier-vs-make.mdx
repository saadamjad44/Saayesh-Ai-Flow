  - question: What is the main difference between n8n, Zapier and Make?

Comparisons of these three usually open with integration counts and close with a recommendation that happens to match whoever wrote it. The integration count is close to irrelevant once you have checked that your own handful of apps is covered, and the thing that will actually decide your bill is further down most pages, if it appears at all.

So this one starts with billing, because it is where the three genuinely diverge — not by a few percent, but by a multiple.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **They bill on different units:** Zapier per task (each action step), Make per operation (each module run), n8n per execution (one whole workflow run).
- **That means a five-step workflow costs five units on Zapier and one on n8n,** which is the real source of the cost gap people report.
- **Zapier wins on app coverage and ease;** Make on visual multi-branch logic; n8n on flexibility, AI work and self-hosting.
- **Self-hosted n8n is the cheapest option and the only one with an ops cost** — free licence, your server, your 2am debugging.
- **There is no clean migration path between them,** so choose for the volume you expect, not only the volume you have.

</KeyTakeaways>

This single row explains most of the dramatic cost comparisons you will read. They are not marketing exaggerations, but they are not universal either — they are a consequence of **step count**, and they only apply if your workflows are long.

Do the arithmetic for your own case before anything else:

1. **Count the action steps** in your typical workflow. Not the trigger — the actions after it.
2. **Estimate how many times a month it runs.** Use your busy month.
3. **Multiply.** Steps × runs is roughly your Zapier task consumption and your Make operation consumption. Runs alone is your n8n execution count.
4. **Compare that against each vendor's current tiers.**

If your workflows are two or three steps and run a few hundred times a month, the three will land close together and you should choose on ease of use. If you are running ten-step workflows thousands of times a month, the per-step platforms get expensive quickly and the gap is the whole decision.

<Callout tone="warning" heading="Ask how failures are billed">
  A workflow that errors halfway through has still consumed the steps it completed. On per-step
  billing, a flaky integration that retries can quietly cost real money, and retry behaviour differs
  between the three. Check it before you commit — it is rarely on the pricing page, and it is the
  detail that makes a busy month expensive.
</Callout>

## What else actually differs

### App coverage

Zapier connects to the most apps by a clear margin, Make is next, n8n has the fewest ready-made integrations but can call any HTTP API, which covers most gaps if you are willing to read documentation.

The useful way to assess this is not the headline number. **Write down the five apps you actually need connected and check each platform for those five.** A platform with 7,000 integrations that lacks your booking system is worse for you than one with 400 that has it. For most small businesses using mainstream software, all three will cover the list.

### The learning curve

Zapier is the easiest to start with: linear workflows, plain-language setup, and a large template library you can adapt rather than build from nothing. For a first automation this matters more than anything else on this page.

Make puts your workflow on a visual canvas, which is genuinely clearer once logic branches — two paths, conditional routes, loops over lists. The canvas is the reason to choose it, and it is a real reason.

n8n expects more of you. The trade is control: more capable branching and data handling, and a model where complexity does not increase your bill.

### AI steps

All three can call an AI model inside a workflow. The differences that matter:

- **n8n** goes furthest, with purpose-built nodes for agent-style loops and retrieval work, and no per-step penalty for the extra calls those patterns need.
- **Make** sits in the middle with straightforward model modules.
- **Zapier** covers the common cases — summarise, classify, draft — which is genuinely most of what a small business needs.

Note the interaction with billing. AI patterns are step-heavy by nature, so the platform that does not charge per step is structurally better suited to them. If AI steps are the point of your automation rather than a garnish, that is an argument for n8n beyond the price.

### Self-hosting

This is n8n's distinctive feature and the source of its biggest savings: it is open-source, so you can run it on your own server with no licence fee and no execution cap.

The cost does not disappear, it changes shape. You own updates, backups, uptime and debugging. A broken automation at the wrong moment is now your problem to fix rather than a support ticket.

That is a good deal if you are technical and running real volume. It is a bad deal if nobody owns the server, and "I'll learn it" is not ownership. Both Zapier and Make are cloud-only, and n8n also sells a cloud plan if you want its billing model without the operations.

## Switching, and why to think about it now

There is no meaningful portability between these platforms. Workflows get rebuilt, not migrated, and the rebuild costs roughly what the original build cost.

Two practical conclusions. **Choose partly on the volume you expect**, not only the volume you have today, because the switch is expensive enough that people stay on an overpriced plan rather than face it. And **document what each workflow does** — trigger, steps, what it touches, why it exists — so that if you do switch, you are translating a specification rather than reverse-engineering your own past decisions.

Do not, however, switch to save money you are not currently spending. A cheaper platform you have not learned is not cheaper.

**Does one of your apps already do this?** Support platforms, email suites, meeting tools and workspace apps increasingly include the AI step you were about to build. A built-in feature needs no connection to break. The [AI automation and workflows](/ai-automation/) hub covers how to tell.
