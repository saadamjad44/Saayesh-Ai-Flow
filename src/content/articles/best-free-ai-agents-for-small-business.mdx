Every honest answer to "what is a free AI agent" sits in one of these three categories. Reading them in order explains why most vendor pages that call themselves free do not actually fit any of them.

Every product that calls itself free and does not fit one of the three above is doing something else. Common patterns worth naming:

- **Credit-card trial dressed as a free plan.** You click "Start free", the page asks for a card, the plan runs for 14 days. That is a paid product with a trial.
- **Lifetime free of a product nobody uses.** A free plan with limits so tight that no business workload fits, kept available to appear in SERP for "free". Technically free, functionally not.
- **Free for personal use, not for business.** A terms-of-service exclusion that makes business use a violation of the free plan, with the business tier priced separately.

None of these is dishonest; they are all common pricing patterns. The question is just whether what you are looking at matches your definition of free.

## Open-source agents, when they make sense

The open-source category is the only one where "free forever" is literally true, and it is also the category most oversold on freedom. The right way to think about it is that you have replaced the licence cost with your own time, and whether that trade works depends on how expensive your time is.

The honest shape of an open-source agent setup:

**Day 1.** Install the framework (LangChain, AutoGen, CrewAI, n8n self-hosted), wire up API keys to a model vendor's lowest plan, connect the one or two apps you need the agent to reach, run it on a test case, fix the five things that break the first time, and get a working run.

**Day 2 onwards.** Watch it in production for a week, fix the two things that only show up at scale, and move on.

**Day N, quarterly.** A connected app changes its API, or a model vendor deprecates a feature, or an SDK version breaks something, and you spend a day bringing the stack back to working. This is the real cost, and it is not optional.

That shape is a good deal for a technically comfortable owner whose time is already partly on the stack, and a bad deal for an owner who would pay themselves for that day. For most small businesses, a paid tier of a managed product is cheaper than the open-source version once the owner's day a quarter is costed properly.

## Free tiers of general assistants, as agents

The clearest way to understand agent features inside the general assistants is that most of what a small business wants from an "agent" is already in the free or paid tier of the assistant they already use. Scheduled tasks, projects with persistent context, artifacts, and a handful of connected apps are not agents in the research sense - they are not deciding on autonomous actions in the wild. But they are what the agent marketing is often describing, and they are often enough.

The specific features worth knowing about in late 2026:

- **ChatGPT scheduled tasks** on the free tier lets you set recurring prompts that run and surface results. Limits on cadence and number of tasks apply.
- **Claude Projects** on the free tier gives persistent context across conversations, which is the agent-like feature most small businesses actually need - a workspace that remembers your writing style, your product, your clients.
- **Microsoft Copilot** in a Microsoft 365 Business Standard or above subscription includes agent features on top of the subscription you already pay for, and may be the lowest marginal cost option for a business already in the Microsoft stack.
- **Google Gemini** in Google Workspace Business plans includes similar bundled features.

The honest version of the recommendation here is to look at what your existing subscription includes before looking at any dedicated agent product. The agent feature bundled into a tool you already pay for is the cheapest agent you can run, and the data terms are already in place.

## Workflow platforms, when "free" means free-tier

The workflow automation platforms with free tiers that include AI steps are the right place to start if the task has a clear trigger and output - a form submission that needs to generate a summary, a new email that needs to be classified, a scheduled report that needs to be drafted.

The three worth naming:

- **Zapier** - a free plan with 100 tasks per month, limited to two-step Zaps. The AI steps count against the task cap. Enough to prove a workflow; not enough to run it in production.
- **Make** - a free plan with 1,000 operations per month, more generous than Zapier's. Operations are not the same as tasks (a scenario can consume multiple operations per run), so the headline number is more generous than the actual ceiling.
- **n8n** - the cloud plan has a free tier with limits; the self-hosted version is open-source and free of licence, which puts it in both the open-source and workflow categories.

## When paid becomes cheaper than free

Three signals, in the order most small businesses hit them.

**Signal one: planning around limits.** You start building workflows in a shape the free tier can handle, rather than the shape the work actually needs. At that point the free plan is no longer saving money; it is costing capability.

**Signal two: data terms do not cover the use.** A free tier that trains on your data is fine for drafting marketing copy and not fine for customer support. The question is not whether the free tier is cheap; it is whether it is legal for the work you now want to do with it.

**Signal three: workaround time exceeds subscription cost.** An hour a week spent working around a free tier's limits is 52 hours a year. If the paid plan costs less than those hours at your own rate, the paid plan is the cheaper option.

Most small businesses hit at least one of these in the second or third month of real use. When that happens, the paid tier is not an upgrade; it is the realistic version of what the free trial was always going to be.

## What to avoid in the free agent category

Four patterns that cost more than any subscription.

**The all-connected free plan.** A free tier that connects to every app you have will absorb more data than the lowest paid tier of the same vendor, and the free tier is the one with the weakest data terms. If the plan is free and the connection list is long, the trade-off is written somewhere in the terms.

**The "lifetime deal" from a vendor you have not heard of.** A one-time payment for an unknown vendor's free-looking product is not a bargain; it is a bet that the vendor will exist when you need them. The failure rate on this category is significant.

**The agent that writes to production on day one.** Any agent, free or paid, that is wired to send real emails, post real content, or act on real customers before you have seen a week of its outputs in draft mode is a liability. Draft-and-review is the only safe starting mode regardless of what the free tier allows.

**The free plan that requires a credit card.** The friction exists for a reason - the vendor is relying on forgotten subscriptions. If you must sign up, calendar the trial end date before you set up anything else.

## The honest summary

Free AI agents in late 2026 are useful in three specific shapes - open-source for the technical, bundled features for the already-subscribed, and workflow free tiers for the testable - and oversold everywhere else. The agents that solve a real small business workload at scale usually cost something, and the question is whether the cost is in a subscription, in your time, or in the limits of a free tier that no longer fits.

The right starting point for most small businesses is the agent features inside the subscription you already pay for.
