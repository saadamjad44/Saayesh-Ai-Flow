Lists of the best AI automation tools tend to mix four genuinely different kinds of product into one ranking, which makes the ranking meaningless: a platform that connects your apps is not competing with a feature inside your email client, and neither is competing with a general assistant that can run a task on a schedule.

So this guide sorts the field into those four categories first, says what each is for, and gives you a way to work out which one your problem belongs in. That is the part that stays true as products change, and it is the part that decides whether you end up paying for something you did not need.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Four categories, not one ranking:** automation platforms, AI already built into your apps, general assistants with scheduled tasks, and single-purpose tools.
- **Check category two first.** The cheapest automation is usually a feature inside software you already pay for.
- **A platform earns its place when the job spans apps** that do not know about each other. That is the one thing only it can do.
- **Most good workflows are ordinary automations with one AI step,** not end-to-end intelligence.
- **Price the billing unit, not the headline.** Platforms bill on volume and step count; assistants bill per seat.

</KeyTakeaways>

The ordering is deliberate: it is roughly the order of how much work each adds to your life. A built-in feature has nothing to break between systems. A platform workflow has every connection in it as a potential failure point.

### Automation platforms

This is what most people mean by automation tooling. You define a trigger and a sequence of actions, optionally with an AI step for the parts that involve reading or writing text, and the platform runs it.

The three most commonly compared are Zapier, Make and n8n, and they differ more than their feature tables suggest — above all in how they bill you, which can mean a multiple rather than a margin for identical work. [n8n vs Zapier vs Make](/ai-automation/n8n-vs-zapier-vs-make/) covers that comparison properly.

What to compare, whichever you are looking at:

- **Your five apps.** Not the integration count — the specific apps you need connected.
- **The billing unit.** Per step or per workflow run, and what a busy month costs at that unit.
- **How failures are billed and surfaced.** A retrying integration costs money on per-step pricing, and automations fail quietly when an app changes.
- **Error alerting and run logs.** The difference between noticing a break in an hour and noticing it in a fortnight.
- **How much AI it includes** versus needing your own model account.

### AI built into your apps

The category most often skipped, and the one to check first. Email suites, workspace tools, support platforms, meeting recorders, accounting software and CRMs increasingly include the exact AI step you were planning to build — summarise this thread, draft this reply, categorise this expense, transcribe this call.

Two reasons this usually wins when it is available. It costs nothing extra, because you already pay for the software. And it does not break when an API changes, because there is no connection between systems to break.

The way to audit this is unglamorous and effective: list the subscriptions you already pay for, and spend an hour finding out what AI features each one has added. Most people find at least one thing they were about to pay a second time for.

### General assistants with tasks

ChatGPT, Claude and the like have moved past the chat box: they can hold memory, connect to your accounts, and run tasks on a schedule or a trigger. For work that is yours rather than your customers' — a weekly summary, a research digest, a recurring report — this is often enough, and you may already be paying for it.

[Claude vs ChatGPT for small business](/ai-business-tools/claude-vs-chatgpt-for-small-business/) compares the two most common on exactly this kind of operational work, and [Claude for small business](/ai-business-tools/claude-for-small-business/) goes deeper on the connected-workflow side.

Where this category stops: anything that must run reliably and identically every time. Assistants are variable by nature, which is fine for a digest and not fine for an invoice.

### Single-purpose tools

A tool that automates one job and does it properly: meeting notes, receipt capture, social scheduling, review requests. They look narrow next to a platform, and they are frequently the better buy — the workflow is pre-built, maintained by someone else, and does not need designing by you.

Judge these on whether the one job is genuinely a recurring cost to you. If you have four meetings a week, a meeting tool pays for itself. If you have four a month, it does not.

## Working out which category you need

Answer in order and stop at the first yes:

1. **Does the work happen inside one app?** Check that app's AI features. If it does what you need, you are finished and it costs nothing.
2. **Is there a tool that does exactly this one job?** If the job is common — meeting notes, receipts, scheduling — a single-purpose tool is usually less work than building the same thing.
3. **Is the task yours, occasional, and judgement-heavy?** A general assistant with a saved instruction or a scheduled task, probably in a subscription you hold.
4. **Does the job cross two or more apps that do not know about each other?** Now you need a platform. This is the one thing only a platform does.
5. **Does the route through the task change case by case?** Then you may be looking at an agent rather than an automation, which is a different purchase with different economics — see [AI agents vs automation](/ai-agents/ai-agents-vs-automation/).

Most small businesses stop at step one or two more often than they expect, which is the useful finding in this exercise.

<Callout tone="warning" heading="Do the task by hand first">
  Automation multiplies whatever it is given, mistakes included. A process you have not yet done
  manually is a process you do not yet understand well enough to automate, and the result is a
  workflow that reliably does the wrong thing — faster than you could have done it wrong yourself.
  Run it by hand with a good prompt until it is boring, then automate the boring version.
</Callout>

## What to avoid

- **Buying the platform before designing the workflow.** The platform is the last decision, not the first.
- **Paying twice for a feature you already have.** Audit your subscriptions before adding one.
- **Automating the hardest task first.** Start with the one you can verify in seconds, so you can tell whether it is working.
- **Letting an AI step take an action immediately.** Draft first, send later, once a category has earned it.
- **Choosing on integration count.** Check your own five apps instead.
- **Ignoring the failure path.** Ask how you will be told when it breaks, because it will break quietly when an app changes.
- **Forgetting review time.** A workflow that saves twenty minutes and adds fifteen minutes of checking saved five. Our free [AI email time savings calculator](/ai-automation/ai-email-time-savings-calculator/) does that arithmetic for email work.
