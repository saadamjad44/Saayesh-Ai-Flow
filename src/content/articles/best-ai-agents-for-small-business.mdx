## Decide the category first

Almost every bad agent purchase is a category error rather than a product error — the right type of tool was never under consideration. Four categories are worth knowing, and they are not really competitors.

## The questions that actually separate products

Once you are in the right category, these are what distinguish one product from another. Ask them in this order — the early ones disqualify faster.

### 1. How am I billed, and what counts as a unit?

This is first because it is the hardest thing to undo. Per-seat software costs the same in a busy month. Agent products are commonly priced per resolved conversation, per run or execution, per action taken, or against credits that different operations consume at different rates — sometimes with a platform fee on top.

So ask, specifically:

- What is one billable unit, in plain words?
- How are **failed** runs counted?
- How are runs **escalated to a human** counted? A support agent that hands over a third of conversations may or may not charge you for them, and the answer changes the economics completely.
- Is there a cap, an alert, or an overage rate?

Then price your **peak** month at the top of your expected range. A support agent costs most during the week something has gone wrong, which is the week you are least likely to be reading invoices. Once you have real quotes, our free [small business AI cost calculator](/ai-business-tools/small-business-ai-cost-calculator/) will total the stack alongside the rest of your subscriptions, and [how much does AI cost for a small business](/ai-business-tools/how-much-does-ai-cost-small-business/) sets out the wider budget picture.

### 2. Can it run in draft or approval mode?

Non-negotiable for anything customer-facing. You want the agent preparing work you release, at least until it has earned more than that. Ask whether approval can be set per action — drafting a reply alone, but asking before issuing a refund — because all-or-nothing autonomy is far less useful than it sounds.

A product that cannot draft is telling you what stage it is at.

### 3. What does it do when it does not know?

The single most informative question you can ask, and the one most likely to be answered vaguely. You are looking for an explicit, configurable behaviour: say it does not know, escalate, and stop. The failure mode you are guarding against is not silence — it is a confident, plausible, wrong answer, delivered to a customer in your name.

Ask to see that behaviour in a trial, on a question your help centre does not cover. Make up something your documentation genuinely does not answer and watch what happens.

### 4. What can I see afterwards?

You need a log of what the agent did and why: which sources it used, which tools it called, what it decided. Without that, debugging is guesswork and you cannot answer a customer who asks why they were told something.

### 5. Where does my data go?

Is it used to train models, where is it stored, how long is it kept, and can you delete it? For customer information this is not a preference, it is an obligation, and the answer varies sharply by vendor and by plan tier — the same product's free tier and business tier often have materially different terms.

### 6. What happens when my other apps change?

An agent's usefulness lives in its connections. When your CRM changes an API or your help centre is restructured, does the agent degrade loudly or quietly? Will you be told? Quiet degradation is the characteristic way these setups rot.

### 7. How do I turn it off?

Mid-run, immediately, and without losing the record of what it had already done. Ask before you need it.

## What a sensible first purchase looks like

1. **Name one task, and make it a verifiable one.** Not your most painful task — the one where you can look at the output and know in seconds whether it is right.
2. **Do that task by hand for a week, with a good prompt.** You cannot grade an agent against a standard you have not established. [AI prompts](/ai-prompts/) covers briefing a model properly.
3. **Pick the category, then shortlist two products.** Two, not six. Beyond two you are comparing marketing pages rather than behaviour.
4. **Trial both on the same ten real cases**, including two you expect to go wrong. The awkward cases are the entire point of the trial.
5. **Keep it in draft mode and read-only** for the trial, on one mailbox or one help centre.
6. **Count review time as cost.** An agent that saves twenty minutes and adds fifteen minutes of checking saved you five. Our [AI email time savings calculator](/ai-automation/ai-email-time-savings-calculator/) does this arithmetic for email work.
7. **Decide on evidence, then widen one setting at a time.** More autonomy, more volume, or more access — never two at once.

## When not to buy one at all

Four situations where the honest recommendation is to spend nothing:

**The task is the same every time.** You want an [automation](/ai-automation/). It will be cheaper, faster, more predictable, and it will fail in a way you can see.

**The task is occasional.** A prompt in a tool you already pay for is enough. [ChatGPT prompts for small business tasks](/ai-prompts/chatgpt-prompts-for-small-business/) covers the admin end of this.

**You already pay for a general assistant.** Scheduled tasks, connected apps and memory are increasingly bundled into subscriptions businesses already hold. Exhaust that before adding a line item — [Claude for small business](/ai-business-tools/claude-for-small-business/) and [Claude vs ChatGPT for small business](/ai-business-tools/claude-vs-chatgpt-for-small-business/) cover what those products do today.

**You cannot check the output quickly.** If verifying the work takes as long as doing it, an agent moves the effort rather than removing it, and adds a new failure mode in exchange.

## The honest summary

The agent market in 2026 is genuinely useful at the narrow end and genuinely overclaimed at the broad end. Customer support from a help centre, with escalation, is a real product category that real small businesses run today. An autonomous digital worker that handles a function unsupervised is a demo.

Between those sits the work most owners actually want done — inbox, admin, follow-ups, research — and for most of that the right answer is either the assistant you already pay for or an automation with one AI step in it.

If after all that an agent still fits your task, the shopping list is short: clear billing you have modelled at peak, draft mode you can scope per action, an explicit do-not-know behaviour you have watched, and a log you can read. A product that gives you those four is a reasonable bet whatever its position on somebody else's list.
