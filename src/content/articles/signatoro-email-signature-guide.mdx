Most people type their own name at the bottom of an email and never think about it again. That works until the details start to matter: a client needs your phone number, a prospect wants to check that you are real, a teammate sends a quote carrying last year's job title, or an AI step drafts a reply and invents a signature that belongs to nobody. A signature is small, repetitive, and easy to get subtly wrong in a lot of places at once — which is exactly the kind of problem a tool can solve.

Signatoro is one of the tools built for that job, and it has an unusual angle. As well as producing a signature you paste into a mail app, it can serve the same saved signature back over an API, so an automation or an AI assistant uses the real one instead of guessing. This guide explains what it does, how to set it up, what the plans include, and where the honest limits are.

## What Signatoro is

Signatoro is a web app for creating and storing email signatures. Its own positioning line is "One email signature for humans and AI agents," and that split describes the product accurately: one half is a signature editor with installation guides, the other half is a way for software to read the signature you saved.

In the editor you fill in your details — name, title, company, phone, email, website, LinkedIn and extra links — then choose one of five layouts (Line, Mark, Portrait, Stack and Compact), an accent colour and a font, and upload a photo or a logo. It can send you a test email so you can see the result in a real inbox, and it warns about contrast problems in dark mode, which is where a lot of hand-built signatures fall apart.

The output is not a plugin that lives inside your mail app. It is a block you copy — as formatted text, as HTML, or as plain text — and paste into whichever apps you send from. Signatoro publishes separate installation pages for a long list of them, including Gmail, the various versions of Outlook, Apple Mail, iCloud Mail, Yahoo Mail, Proton Mail, Zoho Mail, Fastmail, Thunderbird, HEY, Superhuman, Spark, and shared inboxes such as Front, Missive, Zendesk and Help Scout.

## What an email signature is, and why it matters

An email signature is the fixed block at the end of a message that identifies the sender. It is not the sign-off line itself — "Thanks" or "Best regards" is part of what you wrote — but the identity underneath it.

Here is the same reply without one:

```
Hi Maria,

Yes, we can take that on next week. I will send the quote tomorrow.

Thanks
Sam
```

And with one:

```
Hi Maria,

Yes, we can take that on next week. I will send the quote tomorrow.

Thanks
Sam

Sam Okonkwo
Lead Joiner | Okonkwo Carpentry
+44 20 7946 0000 | okonkwocarpentry.example
```

Nothing about the message changed. What changed is that the recipient can now phone you, check that the business exists, and forward the thread to a colleague who has no idea who you are. For a freelancer or a small business, that is most of what a signature is for.

The trade-off is restraint. A signature running to six lines, three social icons, a quotation and a legal disclaimer gets collapsed by mail clients and breaks on phones. Name, role, one phone number and one link is usually the whole job.

- **Per-app installation guides** for several dozen mail apps, shared inboxes and helpdesks.

This sequence works whether you build the signature in Signatoro or by hand. The point is to decide the content before you start choosing fonts.

## How to use Signatoro with Gmail

Signatoro's Gmail page documents the standard Gmail route, which is a paste into Gmail's own settings:

1. In Gmail, click the **Settings** gear at the top right, then **See all settings**.
2. On the **General** tab, scroll to **Signature** and click **Create new**, type a name for it, and click **Create**.
3. Paste the signature into the box on the right, as formatted text, exactly as "Copy signature" copied it.
4. In the **signature defaults**, choose the new signature for new emails _and_ for replies.
5. Click **Save changes** at the bottom of the page.

The limitations here are Gmail's, not Signatoro's, and they are worth knowing before you blame the tool:

- **Gmail's own cap is 10,000 characters.** A normal signature is nowhere near it.
- **Images need a public web address.** Signatoro's documentation notes that Google Drive images only work if they are shared publicly.
- **Gmail may hide the signature behind the three dots in a thread,** so a signature is not a reliable place to put something the reader must see.
- **The Gmail mobile app has its own signature setting.** Signatoro's guidance is to leave the mobile signature empty so the desktop one is used and the formatting survives.

<Callout tone="note" heading="Gmail already does this for free">
  Gmail has a built-in signature feature at no extra cost, and Google's help pages document multiple
  signatures, separate defaults for new mail and replies, a different signature per "send mail as"
  address, and the 10,000-character allowance. If you use only Gmail, need only one signature, and
  are happy maintaining it yourself, Signatoro is optional. It starts to pay off when there are
  several mail apps, several people, or an automation that needs to read the signature

## Signatoro for AI agents and automation

Here is the problem the API is aimed at. In an AI drafting workflow the AI step writes the body of a reply, and then that reply has to be signed. Asking the model to sign it means asking it to produce contact details, which it will sometimes invent. Hardcoding your signature into the prompt instead works until a detail changes, and then you are editing prompts inside automations you had forgotten you built, on every platform, one at a time.

The fix is to keep the two jobs apart: the AI writes the words, and something else supplies the signature. There are four places that something else can be.

### The documented API

Signatoro documents a single endpoint for fetching a signature:

```http
GET https://signatoro.com/api/v1/signature
Authorization: Bearer YOUR_API_KEY
```

Two optional query parameters are documented: `mode`, which takes `full` (the default) or `short` for a cut-down cold-outreach version, and `signature`, which defaults to `me` and otherwise takes an agent id or name. A second endpoint, `GET /api/v1/signatures`, lists the signatures a key can read.

The documented success response is JSON:

```json
{
  "html": "<table>...</table>",
  "text": "Sam Okonkwo\nLead Joiner\n...",
  "sender": {
    "name": "...",
    "title": "...",
    "company": "...",
    "email": "...",
    "phone": { "display": "...", "e164": "..." },
    "website": "...",
    "linkedin": "...",
    "bookingUrl": "..."
  },
  "signature": { "kind": "me", "name": "..." },
  "mode": "full",
  "tier": "...",
  "usage": "..."
}
```

For an email workflow, `html` is the field you want: it is the signature markup, ready to go after an HTML message body. Use `text` when the workflow sends plain text, and `sender` when you need the individual fields rather than the rendered block.

The documented errors are worth handling rather than ignoring: `401 invalid_api_key`, `404 no_signature` when nobody has saved one, `403 identity_unavailable`, and `429 quota_exceeded`. That last one matters most in practice — free accounts get 50 API or MCP requests a month and paid plans are unlimited, so a live workflow on a free key will start failing partway through the month.

<Callout tone="warning" heading="Keep the key out of your prompts">
  The API key is generated once on your signature page, under "For tools and assistants", and
  Signatoro's documentation says the page shows it only once and that it should be stored in
  environment variables and kept out of prompts and logs. Put it in your automation platform's
  credential or secret store — never in the text of an AI step, a shared document, or a committed
  file.
</Callout>

## Signatoro with Zapier, step by step

A practical scenario: a labelled email arrives in Gmail, an AI step drafts a reply, and the reply waits in your drafts with your real signature on it, for you to read and send. This is the level-two "automatic drafts" setup from our guide to [automating email responses with AI](/ai-automation/how-to-automate-email-with-ai/) — a person still presses send.

There are two ways to get the signature onto that draft, and Signatoro's automation documentation is explicit that for Gmail the simpler one is usually right.

### Route A: Gmail's own signature default

For Gmail actions in Zapier, Signatoro's automation documentation says to use the Gmail action's **Add signature default** field and select the signature you saved in Gmail. No HTTP request, no API key, and nothing extra to pay for. If you draft from one Gmail account, start here.

### Route B: fetch the signature over the API

The API route is the one Signatoro documents for Outlook in Zapier, and it is what you want when the signature has to come from a central store rather than one mailbox's settings — several senders, several platforms, or signatures managed on behalf of a team.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Trigger: New Labeled Email">
    In Zapier, create a Zap with Gmail as the trigger app and **New Labeled Email** as the event,
    pointing at a label such as "AI draft" that you apply with a Gmail filter or by hand. Test it
    against a real recent email.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Draft the reply with an AI step">
    Add an **AI by Zapier** step and map the sender name, subject and body into it. Give it your
    approved answers, and tell it explicitly not to write a signature — see the instruction below.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Filter out anything that should not get a draft">
    Add a **Filter** step that only continues if the AI output does not contain `ESCALATE`.
    Complaints, refunds and legal questions should reach you unanswered, not pre-drafted.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Fetch the signature with Webhooks by Zapier">
    Add a **Webhooks by Zapier** action with the **GET** event. Set the URL to
    `https://signatoro.com/api/v1/signature` and add a header with the key `Authorization` and the
    value `Bearer YOUR_API_KEY`, taken from your credential store. Test the step and confirm the
    response contains an `html` field.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create the draft reply">
    Add a **Gmail** action with the **Create Draft Reply** event, mapped to the original thread. In
    the body, map the AI step's output first and the webhook step's `html` field after it, so the
    signature lands at the bottom of the message. Make sure the action is set to send HTML.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Switch off the second signature">
    If this route is appending the fetched signature, the Gmail action must not also be adding a
    signature default, or the draft will carry two. Pick one source and disable the other.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test on real past emails, then turn it on">
    Run it against several real messages, including an awkward one and one that should escalate, and
    read every draft. Then turn the Zap on and check its run history and your drafts daily for the
    first couple of weeks.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The AI step's instructions are where the signature problem is actually solved. Tell the model to write the message and stop:

<PromptBlock
  label="Closing instruction for the AI drafting step"
  note="This is the line that stops the AI inventing contact details. The signature is attached by a later step, not by the model."
  prompt={`End with a short professional closing line, but do not generate a name, contact details, or email signature. The signature will be added separately.`}
/>

<Callout tone="warning" heading="Check the plan requirements before you build">
  Zapier's help pages list Webhooks by Zapier as unavailable on the Free plan and available on
  Professional, Team and Enterprise, and multi-step Zaps and AI by Zapier are also listed on paid
  plans — so Route B is a paid-plan workflow on Zapier whichever signature tool you use. Route A
  needs neither the webhook step nor an API key. Check the current plan pages before you commit,
  because plan contents change.
</Callout>

This walkthrough is an illustrative setup assembled from Signatoro's published automation documentation and Zapier's own documentation for the Gmail and Webhooks steps. We have not built or tested this Zap, and field names in Zapier's editor change from time to time.

The same pattern is documented for the other platforms: in n8n, an HTTP Request node with a Bearer Auth credential before the Gmail node, with the node set to HTML and `html` mapped after the body; in Make, an HTTP "Make a request" module with "Parse response" set to Yes, mapping `html` into the Gmail module's signature content field; and in Power Automate, an HTTP action before **Send an email (V2)** with the expression `body('HTTP')?['html']` after your message. Microsoft's HTTP action is a premium connector, so that last route needs a Power Automate Premium licence for the user, or a Process licence assigned to the flow.

## Who Signatoro suits

- **Freelancers and solo operators:** useful if you send from more than one app — a desktop client, webmail and a phone — and want them to match. If you live in Gmail alone, Gmail's free signature is probably enough.
- **Small businesses and teams:** the clearest case. A shared logo, layout, colour and footnote that every signature follows, with each person keeping their own name and photo, is genuinely tedious to maintain by hand, and the Company tiers are priced for small headcounts.
- **AI automation builders:** the reason to look at this rather than a generic signature generator. If you have ever hardcoded a signature into a prompt, one endpoint returning the current one is the right shape of fix.
- **Anyone sending mail from an AI assistant or agent:** the disclosure line on assistant signatures is a sensible default, and increasingly something you will want to be able to show you did.

It is not for you if you want software that answers your email. That is a different category — see our guide to [automating email responses with AI](/ai-automation/how-to-automate-email-with-ai/) and the workflows in [Zapier AI automation examples](/ai-automation/zapier-ai-automation-examples/).

## Alternatives to Signatoro

- **Your mail app's built-in signature.** Gmail, Outlook and Apple Mail all include one at no extra cost, and Google's help pages document multiple Gmail signatures, separate defaults for new mail and replies, and a per-address signature for "send mail as". For one person in one app, this is the right answer and costs nothing.
- **Hardcoding the signature in your automation.** Pasting it into a Zapier step or an n8n node is free and fine for one workflow you rarely touch. It becomes a maintenance problem once there are several.
- **Storing the signature in your own data source.** A row in Airtable, Zapier Tables or a Google Sheet, fetched by the workflow, gives you the same "one place to change it" benefit without a new subscription — but you maintain the HTML yourself, and getting signature HTML to render identically across mail clients is the part people underestimate.
- **Dedicated team signature managers.** Enterprise products apply signatures server-side at the mail gateway, covering every device without anybody pasting anything. They are generally aimed at larger organisations and priced accordingly; check their current pricing directly, since we have not compared them here.

We are not claiming Signatoro is better or worse than any of these. They solve overlapping problems at different prices, and which fits depends on how many mail apps, people and automations you are actually dealing with.

## Final verdict

Signatoro does a narrow job and is reasonably clear about it. The editor is unremarkable in a good way: a clean block, a dark-mode warning, and instructions for installing it in whatever you use. What distinguishes it is the API — a documented GET request returning your current signature as HTML, plus an MCP server for AI clients — which is a sensible answer to a problem AI email workflows really do have.

If you are one person using one mail app, Gmail's free signature already covers you and you can stop here. If you run a small team, or you build AI drafting workflows, the free plan costs nothing to try and the Company tiers are priced at small-business scale. Start free, use Route A if your drafts are created in Gmail, and reach for the API and a paid plan only when the request allowance or the team demands it. What it will not do is answer your email: keep a person reading every draft before it goes out.
