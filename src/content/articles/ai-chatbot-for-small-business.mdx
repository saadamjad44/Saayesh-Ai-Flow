A chat window that answers customer questions is the most commonly sold AI product for a small business, and the one where the gap between the demo and your reality is most predictable. The demo answers beautifully, because the demo has been given good content. Yours will answer about as well as the material you give it.

That is the honest centre of this topic, and it is why this guide spends more time on what a chatbot needs from you than on which one to buy.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **The work is mostly writing, not software.** A chatbot answers from your content, so thin or contradictory answers produce a thin, contradictory bot.
- **It is a support tool first.** Answering questions that already have answers is where it earns its place; selling and advising are where it disappoints.
- **Insist on three things:** a visible route to a human, an honest "I don't know", and transcripts you can read back.
- **Check the billing unit, not the headline price.** Per-conversation and per-message pricing scale with your traffic, so model your busiest month.
- **Start free, on one narrow topic.** You are testing whether customers use it at all, which is cheaper to find out than to assume.

## What it actually does

Strip the marketing away and a support chatbot does four things in sequence:

1. **Reads the question** and works out what is being asked, in whatever wording it arrives.
2. **Searches your content** — help centre, FAQs, uploaded documents, sometimes your product pages — for the passage that answers it.
3. **Writes an answer** based on what it found, in your tone if you have configured one.
4. **Decides what to do next:** answer, ask a clarifying question, capture a lead, or hand over to you.

Step two is the one that determines quality, and it is entirely about your material. This is why two businesses can buy the same product and get completely different results. [AI customer service automation](/ai-automation/ai-customer-service-automation/) covers that pipeline in more detail, including what happens when the search step finds nothing.

Note what is _not_ in that list: knowing anything about your business that is not written down somewhere it can reach. A chatbot has no access to the pricing exception you agreed verbally, the stock that just sold out, or the policy you changed last week in your head.

## What it needs from you before it will work

**Written answers.** Not necessarily a formal help centre — an organised FAQ page or a few uploaded documents is a fine start. But they must exist, and they must agree with each other. Two pages giving different delivery times will produce a bot that gives both.

**One source of truth per question.** If your returns policy appears in three places with slightly different wording, fix that before you connect anything. The bot will faithfully reproduce your inconsistencies.

**An explicit list of what it must not answer.** Anything involving a price you negotiate, a legal or medical question, a complaint, or a promise about a date. These should route to you by rule rather than by the bot's judgement.

**A decision about tone.** Short and factual travels better than chatty. Customers contacting support want the answer, not personality.

  Buying a chatbot to avoid writing your help centre. It does not work in that order, and the
  disappointment gets blamed on the software. If you do the writing first you will find a useful
  side effect: a good number of those questions stop arriving at all, because the answers are now

## Where a chatbot genuinely helps

- **Repeat factual questions.** Opening hours, delivery times, what is included, whether you cover an area. High volume, zero judgement, instantly checkable.
- **Out-of-hours cover.** The clearest unarguable win. A reasonable answer at 11pm beats a good answer at 9am for most simple questions.
- **Triage before you arrive.** Collecting the order number, the problem, and the context so the conversation starts usefully when you pick it up.
- **Pointing people to the right page.** Often more valuable than answering, and much harder to get wrong.
- **Lead capture on simple enquiries.** Qualifying what someone wants before asking for contact details.

## Where it disappoints

- **Selling.** A bot can answer questions about a product. Persuading someone to buy it, or handling a hesitation, is not the same job and it shows.
- **Anything bespoke.** Quoting custom work, scheduling around constraints, or advising on which option suits someone. The judgement is the product, and it is the part that cannot be delegated.
- **Upset customers.** Speed is not what someone wants when they are annoyed. Route these to a person immediately and without a maze.
- **Anything time-sensitive it cannot see.** Stock levels, today's availability, the status of an order — unless the bot is genuinely connected to that system, it will guess, confidently.
- **Questions your content answers badly.** Which is most of them, in most businesses, before the writing is done.

## How the pricing works

No figures here, because these products reprice frequently and a stale number is worse than none. What is stable is the **shape** of the billing, and there are three:

- **A flat monthly tier with a cap** on conversations or messages. Predictable, which is its main virtue. Check the overage rate and what happens at the cap.
- **Per resolved conversation.** Your bill tracks your traffic. Ask what counts as resolved — specifically, whether a conversation that ended in handover to you is billed.
- **Credits per message.** Hardest to forecast, because a long back-and-forth consumes more than a single answer.

The two questions that decide what a bad month costs are how escalations are counted and whether there is a cap you can set. Ask both before you sign up. ## How to add one to your website

1. **Write or tidy the answers first.** Ten good answers beat a hundred vague ones.
2. **Create the account and point it at your content.** Most products crawl a site or take uploads.
3. **Set the boundaries.** What it must not answer, and what it says when it does not know. Make "I don't know, here is a person" an explicit instruction — it is rarely the default.
4. **Customise lightly.** Name, greeting, colours. Keep the greeting specific: "Ask me about delivery, returns or opening hours" outperforms "Hi, how can I help?" because it sets a scope the bot can actually meet.
5. **Paste the embed snippet** into your site before the closing `</body>` tag, so the launcher appears on every page. On a hosted site builder, use its custom-code or third-party-app field.
6. **Test with your ten most common questions,** then with three awkward ones you expect it to fail. The failures are the point of the test.
7. **Read the transcripts weekly** for the first month. This is the whole job, and it is where you find both the wrong answers and the questions you did not know people had.

## What to insist on, whatever you buy

- A **visible, one-click route to a human** that is never hidden behind a loop.
- An **honest "I don't know"** you can configure and have watched working on a question your content does not cover.
- **Transcripts you can read back**, far enough into the past to be useful.
- **Clear data terms** covering whether conversations train models, where they are stored, and for how long.
- **A cap or an alert** on spend, if billing tracks volume.
- **An off switch** you can reach quickly.

## Is a chatbot even the right answer?

Three alternatives worth weighing first, because each is cheaper and each is sometimes correct:

**A better FAQ page.** If the questions are predictable, publishing the answers clearly removes a good share of them with no subscription and no failure modes. Do this regardless — it is also what a chatbot would need.

**An automation on your inbox instead.** If enquiries arrive by email rather than through your website, drafting replies for your approval may fit better than a live chat window. See [how to automate email responses with AI](/ai-automation/how-to-automate-email-with-ai/).

**A person.** If volume is low and the questions need judgement, a chatbot adds a layer without removing the work. [AI tools vs a virtual assistant](/ai-business-tools/ai-tools-vs-virtual-assistant/) covers that trade-off properly.

If you do go ahead, [AI customer service automation](/ai-automation/ai-customer-service-automation/) explains the machinery behind the chat window and how to design the escalation, and the [AI agents](/ai-agents/) hub covers what changes when you let that software take actions rather than just answer.
