Ask a vendor the difference between an AI agent and an automation and you will usually get an answer about capability: agents are the newer, smarter, more powerful thing, and automations are what we had before. That framing is useful if you are selling agents and misleading if you are buying one.

The real difference is narrower and more useful than that, and it decides which tool will serve you. It is a question of who decides the steps.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **The difference is who decides the steps.** An automation runs the sequence you wrote, in your order, every time. An agent is given a goal and works out the route itself at run time.
- **Everything else follows from that.** Predictability, cost, how you debug a failure and how much supervision it needs are all consequences of that one distinction.
- **Most tasks people want an agent for are repeatable enough for an automation,** which is cheaper to run, easier to audit, and fails loudly instead of quietly.
- **The strongest real shape is both:** a rule-based automation with one AI step inside it for the part that needs judgement, and a person reviewing before anything irreversible.
- **If you cannot write your task down as steps,** neither tool is the answer yet.

</KeyTakeaways>

## The one distinction that matters

An **automation** is a sequence you defined. When a form is submitted, add a row to this sheet, send that email, post to this channel. The software's job is to follow your instructions faithfully. It has no view on whether the steps are sensible, and it will run them identically on the thousandth occasion as on the first.

An **AI agent** is given an objective, a set of limits, and access to some tools. Answer this customer's question using our help centre. Reconcile these invoices against the bank feed. Its job is to work out how, each time it runs. It may look something up, read what came back, decide that was not what it needed, and try a different route.

That is the whole of it. Everything else people list as a difference is a downstream consequence:

The row worth sitting with is how each one fails. An automation that breaks tends to stop and tell you. An agent that has misunderstood the task carries on and hands you something that looks finished. That asymmetry is why an agent costs more to supervise than its run price suggests, and why "we will check the output" has to be a real process rather than an intention.

## Where AI steps fit, which is neither

There is a third thing in the middle, and it is where most useful work actually happens today.

An **AI step** is a single model call inside an automation you designed: classify this message, summarise this transcript, pull the delivery address out of this email, draft a reply. The step has judgement. The workflow around it does not — you still wrote the trigger, the routing, the filters, and what happens next.

This is the shape most small businesses should reach for first, because it isolates the unpredictable part. The flexible bit is one node you can inspect, and the automation around it provides the boundaries. Our [Zapier AI automation examples](/ai-automation/zapier-ai-automation-examples/) are mostly built this way, and [how to automate email responses with AI](/ai-automation/how-to-automate-email-with-ai/) walks through one end to end.

So the three options, in increasing order of how much can go wrong:

1. **Rule-based automation.** No judgement anywhere. Predictable.
2. **Automation with an AI step.** Judgement in one place you chose, inside boundaries you set.
3. **Agent.** Judgement about what to do at all, within limits you configured but did not script.

Most people searching for option three want option two.

## A test for your own task

Forget the categories for a moment and look at the work itself.

1. **Write the task down as numbered steps,** as if briefing a new starter who will do it tomorrow.
2. **Count how often you wrote "it depends".** This is the measurement that matters.
3. **Interpret the result:**
   - **Clean steps, no "it depends".** You need an automation. Build that, and do not let anyone
     sell you an agent for it.
   - **One or two "it depends", always in the same place.** You need an automation with an AI step
     at that point. This is the most common honest answer.
   - **"It depends" throughout, and the order changes case to case.** An agent may genuinely fit —
     go on to the next question.
   - **You could not write the steps at all.** Neither tool is ready for this task. Do it by hand
     until you understand it; [how to write effective AI prompts](/getting-started/how-to-write-effective-ai-prompts/)
     covers briefing a model for work you are still learning.
4. **Then ask the supervision question:** can you look at the finished output and know within
   seconds whether it is right? If not, an agent is a bad fit however variable the task is, because
   you will not be able to afford to check it.

<Callout tone="note" heading="Why the frequency question comes second, not first">
  High volume is a reason to automate, not a reason to use an agent. In fact volume argues against
  an agent: the per-run cost is higher, and sampling output quality gets harder as the number of
  runs grows. Variability is what argues for an agent. A task that is both highly variable and very
  high volume is the hardest case of all, and usually the signal to change the process rather than
  to delegate it.
</Callout>

## Worked examples

The same business function can need a different answer depending on how the work actually arrives. These five are deliberately chosen to show that.

**Invoice chasing — automation.** On day 30, if the invoice is unpaid, send reminder one. Day 45, reminder two. Day 60, flag it to you. No judgement, no variation, and nothing an agent would improve. A rule-based workflow does this perfectly for a fraction of the cost.

**Customer support first replies — agent.** Questions arrive in wording you cannot predict, the answers already live in your help centre, and anything the agent cannot handle has an obvious escalation path. This is the clearest genuine agent case there is.

**Meeting notes into tasks — automation with an AI step.** The trigger (recording finished), the destination (your task list) and the format are fixed. Only "what were the action items" needs judgement. One AI step, inside a rule-based workflow.

**Weekly social posts from a new article — automation with an AI step.** Same shape. Fixed trigger and destination, one step that needs to write. The reason this is not an agent is that nothing about the route varies; only the words do.

**Quoting a bespoke job — neither, yet.** The variability here is not in the steps, it is in your judgement and your pricing knowledge, much of which has never been written down. Until it is, no agent can reach it. Do it with a prompt and your own notes, and revisit when the knowledge exists somewhere a tool can read. ## Cost, honestly

A rule-based run is a few cheap operations. An agent run involves repeated model calls while it reasons about what to do next, and may call several tools before it finishes. Agent products are then commonly billed per run, per action taken, or per conversation resolved rather than per seat.

Two practical consequences:

- **The cost difference is widest exactly where you most want to delegate.** High-volume repetitive work is the cheapest thing to automate and among the most expensive things to hand to an agent.
- **Your bill tracks your busiest month.** With per-seat software a busy month costs the same as a quiet one. With per-run billing it does not, and the spike arrives when you have least time to notice it.

Ask any agent vendor how failed, abandoned and escalated runs are counted before you commit. It is rarely on the pricing page, and it is the number that decides what a bad month costs. ## What about RPA?

Robotic process automation gets folded into this comparison and does not belong in the same column as either.

RPA drives software through its interface — replaying clicks and keystrokes against screens built for people. It is rule-based, so it sits with automations on the predictability question, but it is far more brittle than an API-based workflow: a redesigned screen or a moved button breaks it.

The contrast with an agent is useful precisely because they fail in opposite ways. RPA breaks loudly and predictably when the world changes. An agent adapts to the changed world and may quietly do something you did not intend. If you have the choice, an API-based automation beats both for the work either could do.

## The order to do this in

1. **Do the task by hand with a good prompt.** You learn what a correct result looks like, which you need before you can grade anything. [AI prompts](/ai-prompts/) covers this properly.
2. **When it gets boring, automate the predictable part.** Trigger, routing, recording. [AI automation and workflows](/ai-automation/) is the hub for this.
3. **Add an AI step where the judgement actually sits.** One place, inspectable, with a human review after it.
4. **Only consider an agent if the route itself keeps varying.** And then start it in draft mode on one narrow task, as described in our [AI agents](/ai-agents/) overview.

Skipping to step four is the expensive mistake, because it means delegating a process you have not yet understood at the layer that is hardest to debug.
