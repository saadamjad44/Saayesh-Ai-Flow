**Mostly yes, for most of the work — and the honest version of the answer is that "safe" is not a property of ChatGPT at all. It is a property of the pairing: which data, in which account.** The same prompt is fine on a free plan and reckless on Enterprise, depending on what is in it.

The question is usually asked as though there were a single setting that settles it. There is not. There are five separate places your data can end up, only two of which anybody writes about, and one of them has a court docket attached.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Training is the famous risk and the smallest one.** It is a toggle on personal plans and off by default on business tiers.
- **"Not trained on" is not "not retained".** Deleted chats ordinarily go within about 30 days, and a limited copy survives for abuse monitoring regardless of your training setting.
- **A court can override your deletion settings,** and in the New York Times case it did — to Free, Plus, Pro and Team logs, with Enterprise, Edu and zero-retention API customers outside the order.
- **Turning training off is not retroactive.** It applies to new chats, not to the year of history behind them.
- **Share links are public to whoever holds the URL,** and roughly 4,500 of them were indexed by Google before the option was withdrawn in August 2025.
- **The biggest exposure is the account, not the model.** Verizon's 2026 DBIR puts regular AI use on corporate devices at 45%, up from 15% — and 67% of that access on non-corporate accounts.
- **Four categories should never go in on any plan,** and the list is shorter than most policies make it.

</KeyTakeaways>

## What "safe" has to mean before the question can be answered

"Is ChatGPT safe for business data" packs two questions together, and they have different answers.

The first is whether OpenAI is a competent custodian of data you hand it. On the evidence, broadly yes — the company publishes compliance documentation, offers a data processing agreement on business tiers, encrypts data in transit and at rest, and has not had the kind of repeated breach history that would settle the question the other way.

The second question is whether _your_ use of it is safe, and that one is not about OpenAI at all. It is about whether the data in your prompt was yours to send, whether a contract restricts it, whether the account is one you can answer for, and whether you would be comfortable explaining the prompt to the customer in it.

Almost every page ranking on this query answers the first question and presents it as an answer to the second. That is where the trouble starts, because the first question has a reassuring answer and the second one has your name on it.

## Deleted does not mean gone, and there is a docket to prove it

This is the fact that makes the page worth writing, because it is a real event rather than a hypothetical, and almost nothing written on this query mentions it.

In the consolidated copyright litigation brought by The New York Times and other news organisations, Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang ordered OpenAI on **13 May 2025** to preserve and segregate output log data that would otherwise have been deleted. In plain terms, chats users had deleted, and chats their settings said would be deleted, were kept instead. The going-forward duty ended on **26 September 2025** and the order was lifted on **9 October 2025** — but data already preserved stayed preserved, and accounts flagged by the news organisations remained subject to continuing preservation.

Then it went further. On **5 January 2026**, District Judge Sidney Stein affirmed an order requiring OpenAI to produce a sample of **20 million de-identified user conversations** to the plaintiffs under a protective order. The privacy objection failed, and the reasoning is the part to sit with: the court distinguished the case from wiretap precedent on the basis that ChatGPT users had voluntarily provided their data to OpenAI in the course of ordinary use.

Two things follow for a small business, and they are not the obvious ones.

**Scope is the whole story.** The preservation order covered Free, Plus, Pro and Team logs. Team is the workspace tier OpenAI now calls Business — so a paid company workspace was inside it. ChatGPT Enterprise, ChatGPT Edu and API customers with a zero-data-retention amendment sat outside. The tier that changes the training default did not, on its own, put you outside a preservation order.

**No vendor can promise otherwise.** This is not a criticism of OpenAI, which contested the order publicly. It is a structural point about every AI vendor, and the same would have been true of any of them. A deletion policy is a commitment the vendor makes about its own behaviour, and a court can suspend it.

## Turning training off is right, and it is not retroactive

Do it anyway, today, on every personal account that touches company work. Settings, then data controls, then switch off the option about improving the model for everyone. On the web it is three clicks; on mobile it is in the same place under your profile. Temporary Chat is the per-conversation version — it leaves no history, creates no memory, and is not used for training.

Two limits worth knowing, because both are routinely left out.

**It applies to new conversations only.** Switching it off does not reach backwards into the history already sitting in the account. An account that has been in daily use for a year cannot be made retroactively private by a toggle, which is an argument for doing it now rather than a reason to shrug.

**It does not stop retention.** The training setting and the retention schedule are separate mechanisms. Training off, data still retained for a period, is the normal state of affairs and is not a contradiction.

The reason a workspace helps here has nothing to do with the setting being better. It is that you can see it and answer for it. [ChatGPT Business vs Plus](/ai-business-tools/chatgpt-business-vs-plus/) works through that trade-off and the seat economics behind it; what matters for this page is only that an unverifiable setting on someone else's personal account is not a control at all.

## The two leaks that happen without anyone pasting anything wrong

**Share links.** The share button produces a URL that works for anyone who has it. Until mid-2025 it also offered to make the conversation discoverable by search engines, and the predictable thing happened: reporting found roughly 4,500 shared conversations indexed by Google, containing resumes, internal business material and a good deal people plainly had not meant to publish. OpenAI removed the indexing option in August 2025 and described it as a short-lived experiment that created too many chances to share things accidentally. The indexing is gone. The underlying property is not — a share link is public to whoever holds it, and links get forwarded.

**Personal accounts doing company work.** Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, published on 19 May 2026, found regular employee AI use on corporate devices had risen to 45% from 15% the year before, and ranks shadow AI among the most common non-malicious causes of data leaving an organisation. The figure reported from the same research that should stop a small business owner is this one: 67% of AI access on corporate devices was through non-corporate accounts.

That is the real exposure, and it is not a technical problem. Somebody pastes a customer list into an account you have never seen, with settings you cannot check, on a device you issued. No vendor control touches it. A written rule and a company account do.

## What should never go in, whatever the plan

Short lists get followed. This one has four entries.

- **Anything that identifies a specific customer** — names with contact details, account histories, medical or financial particulars, complaint correspondence. Describe the situation instead of pasting the record; the output is usually better for it, because abstracting forces you to state the actual problem.
- **Anything covered by a client confidentiality or data-protection clause.** The clause will not mention AI and does not need to. This is the same contractual territory as [do you have to tell clients you use AI?](/ai-business-tools/ai-disclosure-to-clients/), and it binds regardless of your settings.
- **Credentials and keys** — passwords, API keys, bank details, anything that would let a reader do something rather than merely know something. These belong in a password manager, and nowhere that keeps a transcript.
- **Anything you could not defend in writing if it surfaced elsewhere.** Staff disciplinary notes, half-formed opinions about a client, a draft of a difficult letter naming people. The preservation order is the proof that "it was deleted" is not always available to you.

Everything else — drafts, plans, summaries of your own material, public information, your own thinking out loud — is ordinary business use of a tool, and treating it as dangerous is its own kind of mistake. The businesses that get this wrong in the cautious direction lose the benefit without buying any safety.

## The three questions to actually answer

If you do nothing else after reading this, answer these in writing. They take ten minutes and they are what a client's security questionnaire is really asking.

1. **Which accounts do company work?** Name them. If the answer is "a few personal ones, probably", that is the finding.
2. **What is on the never-list, in your own words?** Four lines is enough. The hard part is the data list, which is why the [policy template](/ai-business-tools/ai-use-policy-template/) spends most of its length on it.
3. **What would you tell a client who asked?** Write the sentence now, while nobody is waiting for it. Describe what you keep out of the tool, not what the vendor promises to delete.
