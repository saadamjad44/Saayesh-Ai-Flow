**The published figures for how much time AI saves a small business range from a few hours a week to more than twenty.** That is not a disagreement about measurement error. It is an order of magnitude, and the spread tells you more than any of the individual numbers do.

This page is about what those figures are actually measuring, how a headline number is built, what nobody subtracts from it, and how to get a number that is about your business instead of somebody else's.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **The range is an order of magnitude,** which means no single published number can be planned around.
- **Adobe's 175 hours a year is for social media content alone** — one task, self-reported, from a company that sells AI tools.
- **You cannot save time on work you do not already do.** Every headline saving is a hidden claim about your current volume.
- **Almost no published figure subtracts anything** — not setup, not checking, not maintenance.
- **Checking is the cost that never goes away,** and it is the one that decides whether a saving is real.
- **Time saved is not time gained** unless you decide in advance what it is for.
- **Measure your own in a month:** two weeks before, two weeks after, same three tasks, reviewing time included.

</KeyTakeaways>

The pattern is hard to miss once you look for it: the number rises with the commercial interest of whoever published it. That is not an accusation of dishonesty — a business that has genuinely automated a lot does save a lot — it is a reason to read every figure alongside its source. If you want primary data rather than someone's blog post about it, the SBE Council's Small Business Technology Use Survey from March 2026 is the kind of document worth reading directly.

## Why 175 hours is probably not your number

Take the best-documented figure and do the arithmetic on it, because the arithmetic is the whole point.

175 hours a year is about three and a half hours a week. The claim is that AI saves you three and a half hours a week **on social media content**.

Now answer honestly: how much time do you currently spend on social media content? If the answer is three and a half hours a week, the figure might describe you. If the answer is twenty minutes, then twenty minutes is your ceiling — not because the tool is weaker for you, but because you cannot save time on work you were not doing.

That is the test to apply to every time-savings claim you read. **A saving figure is a disguised claim about your current volume.** The number is a statement about the business it was measured on, and it transfers to yours only in so far as your week looks like theirs. A solo consultant who writes two emails and one proposal a day and a six-person agency producing content all week are both "small businesses", and no single hours-saved figure can be true of both.

The same logic cuts the other way, and it is the useful half. If there is a task you do for six hours a week that is repetitive and written down, your potential saving on that one task is larger than any published average — because the averages are flattened across businesses that do not do it at all.

## The three costs nobody subtracts

Published figures are gross. Here is what comes off them.

**Setup.** One-off but real: choosing the tool, learning what it is bad at, writing the instructions properly, building the automation. For a simple task this is an hour. For a workflow with an automation platform in it, budget a day and expect to revisit it twice.

**Checking.** The big one, and the one that never goes away. Anything going to a customer or into a decision has to be read properly — not skimmed, because [confident output and correct output are indistinguishable](/getting-started/what-ai-cannot-do/). If a task took forty minutes and now takes ten minutes of drafting plus eight of checking, your saving is twenty-two minutes, not thirty. Most published figures quietly count thirty.

**Maintenance.** Prompts stop working as models change. Automations keep running after the process they were built around has moved on. Something that worked for three months unattended has often been slightly wrong for one of them. A monthly review of anything customer-facing is the realistic cost, and it belongs in the sum.

Subtract all three and the honest shape of the answer for most small businesses is: a meaningful saving on a small number of specific tasks, not a transformation of the week. Which is still worth having — it is just not what the headline said.

## Measure your own

A month of attention gets you a number worth more than every statistic above, because it is measured on the business you actually run.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Pick three tasks, not a category">
    Specific and repeated — "writing the Monday customer update", not "marketing". If you cannot
    name when it happens and how often, it is not measurable yet.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Time them for two weeks, before changing anything">
    Rough is fine. Start and stop times in a notebook beat a precise system you abandon on day
    three. Most people discover here that one of the three takes half what they assumed, and another
    takes double.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Introduce the tool on those three tasks only">
    Not everywhere at once, or you will not know what caused what.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Time them again for two weeks — including the checking">
    This is the step that makes the number honest. Count review and correction time inside the task,
    because that is where it actually falls.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Subtract the setup, spread over a sensible period">
    An hour of setup against a weekly task is roughly five minutes a week over a quarter. Do not
    pretend it was free, and do not amortise it over five years either.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide what the time is for, in advance">
    Name the thing you will do with it before it appears. Unassigned saved time is absorbed by
    whatever is nearest, and then nobody can tell whether any of it was real.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Time saved is not time gained

The last thing, and it is the one that decides whether any of this shows up in your life.

Twenty minutes freed at three separate points in a day tends to become nothing. The work expands, the inbox fills it, and at the end of the quarter you are equally busy and cannot say where the saving went. This is not a failure of the tools; it is what happens to unallocated time in a business run by one or two people.

So decide first. The saving is worth having if it goes somewhere you chose — the piece of work only you can do, the thing that has been on the list since March, or simply finishing when you said you would. That is a decision about how you work, and no software makes it for you.
