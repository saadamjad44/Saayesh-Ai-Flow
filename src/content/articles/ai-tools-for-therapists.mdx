Most AI-for-therapists content in 2026 is written as though the hard question is which tool to pick. It is not. The hard questions are whether you may use it at all under your regulator's rules, whether the vendor will sign the paperwork that makes client data lawful, and whether the thing you are automating is actually administrative or quietly clinical.

This guide takes those first. It names what AI genuinely helps with in a therapy practice today, what it should not touch regardless of how well the demo runs, and the compliance decisions that come before any software decision. The product recommendations most ranked lists lead with are the last step of this conversation, not the first.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Compliance before capability.** A signed Business Associate Agreement and your professional body's position decide what you may do, not what the tool can do.
- **Administrative work is the safe starting point.** Non-clinical drafting, scheduling, and correspondence that contains no client information is where most practices should begin.
- **Clinical work stays clinical.** Case formulation, treatment planning, and risk assessment are yours to do, and no AI tool has earned a place in that work yet.
- **Consent is a conversation, not a tick-box.** If a tool will be used on a client's session or notes, the client needs to be told how, with what, and for how long.
- **Free-tier assistants are not HIPAA compliant.** Any PHI goes into a plan that has a BAA signed, or it does not go in at all.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Decide what you are willing to automate before you decide the tool

The categories of AI tool sold to therapy practices divide cleanly on one question: does client information go into the tool, or does it not? That decision is more important than the tool choice, because it determines what paperwork needs to be in place before anything starts.

## The questions that actually separate products

Once you are in the right category, these are what distinguish one product from another. Ask them in this order - the first few disqualify faster than the last.

### 1. Will the vendor sign a Business Associate Agreement, and what are the data terms?

First because it decides whether the tool is lawful with PHI at all. Ask for the BAA by name, read the data-use terms attached to it, and note three specific things:

- **Training.** Is practice data used to train the vendor's models, in any form, including de-identified?
- **Retention.** How long is a recording, transcript, or message kept, and can you force deletion on client request?
- **Access.** Who at the vendor can see the data, under what controls, and is that access logged?

A vendor who hesitates on any of these is answering the question. Outside the US, substitute your equivalent - UK GDPR plus the ICO's health-data guidance, the EU's GDPR with any national regime, Canada's PHIPA where it applies - and ask the same three questions. The paperwork has different names; the concern is the same.

### 2. What is your professional body's position on this specific use?

Most psychotherapy and counselling professional bodies have now written guidance on AI assistance, and most of it draws a sharp line between administrative use and clinical use. Before you buy a scribe or anything that touches session content, read your own body's position - ACA, AAMFT, APA, BACP, BPS, UKCP, whichever applies - and treat it as the ceiling on what you may do, regardless of what the tool is capable of.

A vendor's "many therapists use this" is not a regulator's permission. Ask whether the product has been reviewed against your professional body's guidance by anyone other than the vendor, and treat the answer as information about the vendor rather than the product.

### 3. How is consent obtained, and documented?

A client has to know when an AI tool is in use on their work. For an administrative tool that handles identifiable correspondence, consent is a line in the welcome pack and a signature. For a scribe that records a session, it is a conversation at the start of that session and a specific record that the client agreed, including a route to refuse without penalty.

Ask the vendor how consent is captured in the tool itself, what happens if a client later withdraws it, and whether the recording and derived notes can be deleted on request. If the answer is procedural rather than product-level, the discipline sits on you.

### 4. How is the draft verified, and what does the audit trail show?

For anything the AI writes that enters a client record, you need a log of what the AI produced and what you changed. That audit trail is what makes the note defensible later, and it is also the honest record of how reliable the tool actually is on your work.

Review a week of drafts closely. If editing takes longer than writing from scratch, the tool is not saving you time, it is moving the effort - and for clinical documentation, moving the effort in exchange for a worse record is a bad trade.

### 5. How is it billed, and what counts as a billable unit?

Dental AI pricing has the same traps therapy AI has: per seat, per client, per transcribed minute, per message handled, per credit pool. Ask what one billable unit is in plain words, how failed runs are counted, and how escalated or deleted sessions are charged. Price your peak month - the week a wave of new intakes comes in - not your average.

### 6. What happens when the client's situation changes?

Risk, safeguarding, and significant clinical change are not events an AI tool should silently roll into a routine note. Ask the vendor what happens when a session contains content the tool is not supposed to summarise - a disclosure of risk, a reference to a child, anything that would trigger your own escalation process - and whether the tool offers a flag, a stop, or just continues.

### 7. How do you turn it off, mid-session?

Immediately, without losing the record of what it had already produced, and with the client able to see it has stopped. Ask before you need it.

## Where general-purpose assistants fit

A therapy practice can get a real amount of work done with the general assistants it already has access to - [Claude](/ai-business-tools/claude-for-small-business/), ChatGPT, or Copilot through a Microsoft 365 business subscription - provided the bright line is kept between those tasks and anything containing client information.

The jobs that fit this shape, in rough order of safety:

- **Website copy, service descriptions, FAQ pages** for the public, containing no case material.
- **Marketing drafts** - social captions, Google Business posts, newsletter sections - cleared by a human before publication.
- **CPD summaries, literature reviews, journal article digests** that you read anyway and now have less time for.
- **Policy and procedure drafts** - privacy notice, cancellation policy, complaints procedure - with your professional body's framework kept in sight.
- **Replies to non-client enquiries** - suppliers, recruiters, continuing education providers - containing no PHI.

The discipline is the same one that applies in other regulated fields: if a sentence could identify a client to someone who knew that client, it does not go into a prompt. [Claude vs ChatGPT for small business](/ai-business-tools/claude-vs-chatgpt-for-small-business/) covers how the two assistants differ for administrative work, and [AI use policy template](/ai-business-tools/ai-use-policy-template/) covers writing the house rules that keep the line visible.

## What AI should never do in a therapy practice

Four lines that most professional bodies have now drawn, and that are worth drawing in your own practice regardless of whether yours has written them down yet:

**No case formulation.** The hypothesis about what is happening for this client is the clinical work, and a confident, plausible, wrong hypothesis from an AI is a worse starting point than a blank page.

**No treatment planning.** Deciding what to do with a client across sessions is the plan the client is paying for, and it belongs to the clinician who can read the room.

**No risk assessment.** Safeguarding and risk are not an administrative category. A missed cue in an AI-generated risk note is the failure mode that ends a career.

**No AI delivering content to the client as if it were you.** Psychoeducation handouts a client reads with your name on them are ethically yours, not the model's. Drafting is fine; sending without a careful read, in your name, is not.

The common property across the four is that each is work the client is explicitly paying for a qualified clinician to do. AI assistance there is not a productivity gain; it is an outsourcing of the thing the fee is for.

## What a sensible first purchase looks like

1. **Start with no client data.** Pick one administrative task - newsletter, website copy, policy drafts - and use the general assistant you already pay for.
2. **Measure the saving.** Not vibes, minutes. If the task was taking an hour and now takes thirty, that is the baseline. If it still takes an hour because editing matches writing, stop using AI for it.
3. **Decide what you want to automate next.** Only move to the second category - administrative work with PHI - after the first is working, your BAA is signed, and your privacy notice reflects the tool's role.
4. **Scribes come last and come slowly.** If and when a scribe is on the table, consent every client, review every draft for two full weeks, and keep the audit log open.
5. **Treat clinical categories as closed** until your professional body has published guidance that opens them.

## The honest summary

AI in therapy practice in 2026 is useful at the administrative end, cautious at the documentation end, and not yet appropriate at the clinical end. The practices that will benefit are the ones that treat the compliance decisions as the first question rather than the last - who signs the BAA, what the professional body permits, how consent is captured, how the audit trail reads - and that keep the therapeutic work itself human for the simple reason that the therapeutic value is in the relationship, and a relationship with a model is a different product the client did not buy.
