**If everyone on the call is somewhere that allows one-party consent, you can record it. If anyone is in a state that requires all-party consent, you need everybody to agree — and because calls cross borders, the practical rule is to ask every time.**

That is the whole answer, and announcing it costs one sentence. The reason this question is worth a page is not the law; it is that the default settings on these tools let a bot join a meeting silently, and the three risks that bite hardest are not recording law at all.

## The rule in one paragraph

These laws are about recording a conversation, and they come in two shapes. Under **one-party consent**, one participant can agree to the recording — and if you are on the call, that participant can be you, with nobody else told. Under **all-party consent**, everybody has to agree. US federal law and the large majority of states — commonly counted as 39 plus the District of Columbia — take the one-party approach. A minority take the all-party approach.

Nothing in that depends on AI. What AI changed is the volume: recording a call used to be a deliberate act, and now it is the default behaviour of a calendar integration.

The honest point about that table is that no version of it is authoritative. Published counts run from eleven to thirteen states, and they differ because some rules are mixed, some have been read differently by different courts, and some turn on whether the conversation was in person or over a wire. Every vendor page on this topic prints a confident list; the confident lists do not match each other.

Which is why the useful conclusion is not a list at all.

## Why the strictest standard wins

Think about an ordinary call. You are in one state, your client is in another, and somebody has dialled in from a third. A call that crosses jurisdictions can answer to every applicable standard at the same time, and you do not necessarily know where everyone is sitting — somebody is working from a relative's house this week and has not mentioned it.

You cannot run a per-participant legal analysis before a 2pm catch-up. So the only workable policy is to behave as though the strictest rule applies, every time, which means asking. Once that is a habit, the entire question stops being a question.

## The three risks that are not recording law

These are the ones that cause real damage, and none of them is fixed by consent.

**Your client's contract.** A confidentiality or data clause that restricts sending their information to third parties covers the transcript you just sent to a notetaking vendor. The clause will not mention AI, and it does not need to. This is the same problem covered in [do you have to tell clients you use AI?](/ai-business-tools/ai-disclosure-to-clients/) — the data disclosure, rather than the credit one, is the part that binds.

**Privilege.** If a lawyer is on the call, the presence of a third party and the existence of a recording can both matter to whether the conversation stays privileged. Legal practitioners have been warning about exactly this since notetakers became default-on. If a call is genuinely legal advice, the notetaker should not be in it.

**The transcript exists.** This is the one nobody thinks about until it matters. A meeting in somebody's memory cannot be requested, disclosed or leaked. A transcript can, and it contains the throwaway remark somebody made about a customer at minute forty. You have converted a conversation into a record, and records have a life of their own.

## What to say, and what to change

One line, before anything substantive:

> "I've got an AI notetaker on this call taking notes — is that all right with everyone?"

Then stop and let people answer. Asking rather than announcing matters for two reasons: silence is not reliably consent, and the pause is what gives a quieter person room to object.

Then change three settings, today, in whatever tool you use:

- **Automatic joining off.** Many of these tools will put a bot in every meeting on your calendar. That is how a notetaker ends up in a conversation you would never have recorded on purpose.
- **A notice everyone can see.** Some tools show the recording state only to the host. If the people on the call cannot tell, your announcement is the only notice that exists.
- **Retention set deliberately.** Decide how long transcripts live and set it. The default is usually forever, and forever is a decision you did not make.

If someone says no, turn it off and write by hand. If you want to understand the objection, ask whether it is the recording or the vendor — because the second has an answer, in tools that process audio locally rather than sending it to a service.
