    answer: No, and it usually makes the disorder more visible and more expensive.   - question: What decisions should a small business never hand to AI?
    answer: Anything with a genuine trade-off and no correct answer, and anything that carries your name or licence. An assistant can lay out the options and pressure-test your reasoning, which is useful. It cannot hold the consequences, and that is what deciding means.
  - question: Can AI handle customer relationships?
    answer: Parts of them, and not the parts that matter most. It is good at the predictable traffic — order status, opening hours, the same five questions. It cannot take the call where someone is angry and needs to feel heard, decide to waive a fee because this customer has been with you nine years, or apologise in a way that lands. Those are also the moments that decide whether somebody stays, so handing them over trades your best asset for a small saving.
  - question: Is AI set-and-forget once it is working?
    answer: No, and treating it that way is where most of the disappointment comes from.   - question: How do I tell which tasks to give AI and which to keep?
    answer: Four questions. Can someone who knows the subject check the answer in under a minute? Does the task need knowledge that exists only in your head? Does anyone put their name to the result? **The useful question is not what AI can do — every vendor will tell you that — but where it stops.** Knowing the edges in advance is what separates a tool that saves you a day a week from a subscription you cancel in March feeling slightly foolish.

This is the honest list, written for someone running a business rather than evaluating a product. Not "AI lacks empathy", which is true and useless, but the specific limits that decide whether a particular task is worth handing over.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **It does not know what you never wrote down,** and it fills the gap with something plausible instead of saying so.
- **It cannot be accountable.** Anything carrying your name, licence or signature still needs a person behind it.
- **It gives you no signal when it is wrong** — confident and correct come out identical.
- **It cannot fix an undefined process.** Automating a vague process makes it fail consistently rather than occasionally.
- **It cannot make a decision that has a real trade-off in it,** because deciding means holding the consequences.
- **It cannot do the jobs where a human doing it is the point** — the apology, the condolence, the reference.
- **It is not set-and-forget.** Count the ongoing checking against the time saved, or your saving is imaginary.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Why the limits list is the more useful one

Every roundup on this site included, the capability list is easy to write and hard to act on. "AI can help with marketing" does not tell you whether to use it on Tuesday.

The limits list does something the capability list cannot: it tells you where to put the human. A small business does not have spare people, so the question is never "can AI do this" in the abstract — it is "if I hand this over, what do I now have to watch, and is watching it cheaper than doing it". You can only answer that if you know how the thing fails.

## 1. It does not know what you never wrote down

This is the deepest limitation and the one that explains most disappointing output.

Think about what actually runs your business. Which customers pay late but are worth keeping anyway. Why you quietly stopped offering that service two years ago. The standard of finish you will not go below. Which supplier to call when something has gone wrong on a Friday afternoon.

None of that is written anywhere. An assistant works from what it is given, and where your context is missing it does not stop — it produces something reasonable-looking in the shape of the thing you asked for. The output is not wrong because the tool is weak. It is wrong because it was answering a question with a quarter of the information you had in your head when you asked it.

This has a practical consequence worth more than any prompt technique: the quality of AI output in a small business is mostly a function of how much of your own knowledge you are willing to write down. Which is why the businesses that get real value from it tend to end up with better documentation as a side effect.

## 2. It cannot be accountable

There is no version of this that software solves.

If a quote goes out underpriced, a tax position is wrong, a claim in your marketing cannot be substantiated, or advice you gave turns out to be negligent, the consequence lands on you. Your client's contract names you. Your insurer insures you. Your professional body regulates you. "The AI produced it" is not a defence anywhere, and saying it out loud makes things worse rather than better.

So for anything that leaves your business with your name on it, the review step is not optional diligence — it is the thing that makes your position honest. See [do you have to tell clients you use AI?](/ai-business-tools/ai-disclosure-to-clients/) for how to say that to a client in a way that reassures rather than worries them.

## 3. It gives you no signal when it is wrong

Fluency and accuracy come out of the same process, which means they are indistinguishable at the point of delivery. The invented statistic arrives with the same confidence as the real one. The misremembered date is formatted as neatly as the correct one.

<Callout tone="warning" heading="The practical version of this">
  You cannot use "does this sound convincing?" as a check, because sounding convincing is the one
  thing it is guaranteed to do. Check the specifics instead — figures, names, dates, prices,
  quotations, anything that could be looked up. And if you cannot verify something, delete it rather
  than soften it. Hedged wrong information is still wrong information, and the hedge is what lets it
  survive the edit.
</Callout>

## 4. It cannot fix a process that was never defined

Automation needs three things: a defined input, a defined output, and a rule connecting them. If your version of the process is "it depends, I just know", there is nothing to automate — and automating it anyway produces something worse than doing it by hand, because it now does the wrong thing reliably and at volume.

The test is whether you can write the process down in ten steps a new person could follow. If you can, it is a good automation candidate. If you cannot, the thing standing between you and a saving is not software; it is a decision you have not made yet about how the work should go.

This is also why digitising comes before automating. A process whose records live on paper, in a notebook, or in a spreadsheet somebody keeps privately cannot be automated at all, whatever is bolted onto it.

## 5. It cannot make a decision that has a trade-off in it

AI is genuinely good at laying out options, surfacing considerations you had not thought of, and arguing against your own reasoning when you ask it to. All of that is useful before a decision.

What it cannot do is decide, in the cases that matter, because those cases have no correct answer. What to charge. Whether to let a difficult but profitable customer go. Whether to take the loan. Which of two people to promote. Whether to pivot. Every one of those depends on your appetite for risk, your obligations, how long you intend to run this business and what you want your life to look like — and on information nobody has, because it is about the future.

A confident recommendation on a question like that is not insight. It is a guess, delivered in the register of advice.

## 6. It cannot do the jobs where a human doing it is the point

Some communication carries its meaning in the fact that a person chose to spend time on it.

The apology after you got something wrong. The condolence note. The reference for someone who worked for you. The call to a customer of nine years explaining a price rise before they find out from an invoice. The value in all of those is not the words — it is that you wrote them.

AI will produce competent text for every one of these, and using it is a small, hard-to-detect betrayal of the thing the message was supposed to do. It is also the category where being found out is most expensive, because the recipient does not conclude you were efficient. They conclude you did not care enough to type it.

## 7. It is not set-and-forget

The last limit is about time rather than capability, and it is the one that quietly eats the savings.

Prompts that worked stop working as models change. The automation you built around last year's process keeps running after the process moved. An output nobody has checked for three months has often been wrong for two of them, and the longer a thing works unattended the less anyone thinks to look.

Budget the attention: a monthly look at anything customer-facing, a review when a vendor changes a plan or a model, and a note of what the thing is supposed to be doing so that a future you can tell whether it still is. Then subtract that time from the time saved. What is left is the real number — which is the subject of [how much time does AI actually save a small business?](/ai-automation/how-much-time-does-ai-save/)

## What it is genuinely good at

Worth stating plainly, because a limits page read on its own gives the wrong impression. The same honesty that produced the list above says that AI is strong at a specific and valuable set of things: first drafts of anything, turning a messy pile of notes into a structure, summarising a long document you would otherwise not read, rewriting the same information for a different audience, answering the predictable questions that arrive every week, and being a patient sounding board at eleven at night when there is nobody to ask.

For a lot of small businesses that genuinely is a day a week. The reason to know the limits is not scepticism — it is that the day only materialises if you spend it on the tasks where the tool is strong, instead of discovering the hard way which ones it is not.
