**The prompts worth having as an accountant are not the ones that write LinkedIn posts about tax season.** They are the ones that get you through the awkward client conversations - a technical position explained in plain English, a polite chase for the records a client keeps forgetting to send, a scope message when the engagement has drifted past what you quoted.

Those are the exchanges where time disappears. A single scope email can take forty minutes to write and still land wrong. The six prompts on this page cover the conversations where that keeps happening, written to supply the relationship and the context rather than ask you to fill in a blank.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Six prompts, not fifty.** Each is for a job that actually takes hours out of a practice week.
- **Keep client data out of free-tier tools.** Anonymise before pasting, or use a tier whose data terms cover it.
- **Never send the first draft.** The prompt removes the blank page - your judgement is still the whole job.
- **A plain-English tax explanation is not a translation.** It is a rewrite that keeps the position unchanged and cuts the hedging that confused the client in the first place.
- **The chase-for-records email has three jobs,** and most do only one of them, which is why they feel like nagging.
- **Rate rises belong in a separate message**, not buried inside an engagement update, because a bundled rise reads as a defence rather than a notice.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Before you use any of these

Three things that change the output more than any prompt tweak.

- **Give it your own writing.** Paste two or three of your real client emails and tell the assistant to match that voice. Generic prompting produces generic, obviously-AI prose. Your actual style is what distinguishes the draft from the template.
- **Say what you want to happen.** Not "write a reply" - "write a reply that gets the missing bank statements by Friday without implying this is the third time I have asked". The outcome is the instruction.
- **Name what you never say.** This does more work than describing the tone you want. "No 'please find attached', no 'kindly', no listing three reasons in a row" removes more AI-sounding output than "be professional" ever will.

## 1. Explain a technical position in plain English

The job is not translation. It is a rewrite that keeps the position exactly as you would stand behind it, but removes the hedging a client reads as evasion and the jargon a client reads as a bill. The failure mode is simplifying so far that the position shifts - which is how clients act on something you did not actually say.

<PromptBlock
  label="Plain-English technical explanation"
  prompt={`I need to explain a technical tax or accounting position to a client whose business is [describe the business]. They are not an accountant and will read anything over three paragraphs as a bill in waiting.

Here is the position exactly as I would state it to a peer:

[paste the technical note]

Rewrite this as a client email that:
- preserves the position without softening it into something I did not say
- removes qualifiers that exist for a peer audience but read as hedging to a client ("generally", "typically", "in most cases" - keep only where the qualifier changes the answer)
- replaces jargon with the plainest word that still means the same thing
- ends with a single specific action for the client, which is [what you want the client to do next]

Do not add reassurance I did not write. Do not list three benefits. Do not begin with "I hope this finds you well".`}
  note="Any sentence that softens the position - 'you could consider', 'it may be worth' - should be put back the way you wrote it, because the client acts on the softer version and the file keeps the harder one."
/>

**Limitation:** the rewrite depends on the technical note you paste. If that note hedges where you did not mean to, the output will carry the hedge. Write the peer version plainly first, then let the prompt do its job.

## 2. Chase a client for records without sounding like it is the third time

This email does three things and most people only remember the first: it asks for the records, it says what happens if they do not arrive, and it offers a route that is not "send them". Written as nagging, it reads as nagging. Written with the three jobs separated, it does not.

<PromptBlock
  label="Chase a client for missing records"
  prompt={`Write a chasing email to a client for records I need to progress their work. Context on the relationship: [describe the relationship] - adjust the tone accordingly.

The records are [what the records are].

The deadline and consequence: [when the deadline is and what happens if it is missed].

The email must do three things, in this order, in no more than four short paragraphs:

1. State specifically what I need and by when, without recapping how long we have been waiting.
2. State what happens if the deadline is missed - the filing moves, the fee changes, the work stops - stated as a neutral consequence rather than a threat.
3. Offer a route that is not "send them" - a short call to talk through what is held up, or a list of alternative sources (bank export, accountant from a prior year, a letter from HMRC/FBR) I can pursue on their behalf for a stated fee.

Do not apologise. Do not blame. Do not say "I know how busy you are".`}
  note="The third paragraph is the one most chase-emails leave out, and it is the one that changes the response rate. A client with a route that is not 'find the records' will take it."
/>

**Limitation:** this works for records a client has, somewhere. For records a client genuinely does not have, the useful next step is not an email at all - it is a call to decide whether you reconstruct what is reconstructible or scope the engagement down.

## 3. Rewrite a scope message that has started to drift

Scope creep is rarely one big ask. It is six small ones, each reasonable in isolation, and an engagement that was priced for the brief in March doing the work for a brief that now has eleven lines of extras. The honest message says so, offers a route forward, and does not read as a complaint - which is what makes it hard to write cold.

<PromptBlock
  label="Scope-drift message"
  prompt={`I need to write to a client whose engagement has drifted past what we agreed. Here is the original scope: [describe the original scope]. Here is what has been added since: [list what has been added, with dates where possible]. What I want to happen: [describe the outcome you want].

Write a message of no more than five short paragraphs that:
- confirms the extra work is useful and I am happy to do it
- states plainly that it sits outside the original engagement
- gives a price for the addition, or proposes a swap against something already in scope that we can defer
- ends with a single specific question that moves the decision forward

Do not list the items as a complaint. Do not say "going forward". Do not thank the client for their patience.`}
  note="Read the second paragraph for tone: does it read as neutral record-keeping, or as an accusation? If the latter, rewrite that paragraph by hand before sending."
/>

**Limitation:** the prompt cannot tell you whether the addition really is out of scope. If the original scope was vague, no email fixes that, and the honest answer may be to absorb this one and write a tighter scope for the next engagement.

## 4. Pre-engagement questions for a vague brief

A brief that does not say enough to price is a brief that does not say enough to deliver. The expensive mistake is quoting it anyway. The cheap one is a short, friendly message that asks the three or four questions that would let you quote properly - and that reads as professional rather than evasive.

<PromptBlock
  label="Pre-engagement clarification questions"
  prompt={`A prospect has asked me to quote for the following: [paste what the prospect sent]. I provide [describe your service and typical pricing structure].

Write them a short reply - no more than four short paragraphs - that:
- confirms I can help, in one sentence, without over-selling
- asks the three or four questions I need answered to quote properly, as a short list rather than prose
- states the one piece of information they will need from me before engaging (my process, my data policy, my turnaround) so the exchange feels reciprocal
- ends with a specific next step and a suggested time frame

Do not list five selling points. Do not promise a quote within an hour. Do not open with "Thanks for reaching out".`}
  note="A prospect who sees three questions and a next step replies to all four items. A prospect who sees a paragraph with questions buried in it replies to one."
/>

**Limitation:** the questions are the generic set. The specific question for your practice - the one that comes from having been burned by a particular kind of engagement - is still yours to add.

## 5. Decline work without burning the relationship

Saying no is an accounting skill. Capacity is finite, some briefs are a bad fit regardless of fee, and most practices undercharge because the alternative feels like saying no - which it is. The prompt below writes a decline that is short, specific, and points somewhere useful.

<PromptBlock
  label="Decline work politely and usefully"
  prompt={`I need to decline work. The ask was: [paste what was asked]. My real reason is: [state the reason in one honest sentence]. I can offer this as a next step: [state a referral, alternative timing, or "nothing"].

Write a reply, three short paragraphs maximum, that:
- declines clearly in the first paragraph rather than burying it after two of context
- gives a reason that is honest but short - no self-flagellation, no five lines of apology
- offers what I can offer, whether a referral or an alternative timing, in one specific sentence
- closes without a hedge that opens the door to a counter-offer I do not want

Do not say "unfortunately". Do not say "at this time". Do not offer to "revisit this in the new year" unless I have said I actually will.`}
  note="Read the decline sentence in isolation. If the client would not know, from that sentence alone, that the answer is no, rewrite it until they would."
/>

**Limitation:** this decline is for a professional no. For a client who is behaving badly, the useful message is shorter and less relational than this one, and often not an email at all.

## 6. Raise your rate on an existing engagement

Early, briefly, and without an essay of justifications. A long explanation invites a negotiation about each reason in turn, and the longer the message the more negotiable the rate feels. The prompt resists that instinct, because the instinct is strong and it is what weakens the message.

<PromptBlock
  label="Rate rise on an existing client"
  prompt={`Write a rate-rise message to an existing client. Current rate and new rate: [state the old and new rate]. The new rate starts on: [state the effective date]. Relationship context: [describe the engagement].

Three short paragraphs, no more. The message must:
- state the new rate and the date it starts, in the first paragraph, without a preamble
- give one sentence of context that is specific to this engagement rather than generic ("costs have risen" is generic; "the scope we agreed in 2024 has grown by two monthly tasks" is specific)
- close with a line that invites a conversation if the client wants one, without implying the rate itself is up for discussion

Do not apologise. Do not list three reasons. Do not open with "I hope the quarter has gone well".`}
  note="One specific, honest reason costs nothing to concede. A list of reasons gives the client four openings to push back on."
/>

**Limitation:** the prompt cannot tell you whether the new rate is the right one. That number depends on your market, your pipeline, and how much this engagement is costing you to keep at the old rate - and those are not in the prompt.

## What to do with the output

The same discipline applies to every one of these prompts:

1. **Read the draft as the client will read it.** Not as you intended it.
2. **Cut anything that sounds like a policy.** Clients hear policy as "a rule I did not agree to".
3. **Put back the specific detail only you know.** A reference to the project in March, a call three weeks ago, the favour done or asked for - the thing the prompt could not know and that turns a draft into your message.
4. **Keep what you cut.** Save the discarded phrases in a document and reuse the openings and closings that worked. Over six months you will have a short set of lines in your own voice that no prompt could have produced.

And if the output sounds obviously like AI, the quickest fix is not a better prompt - it is the two or three paragraphs of your real emails pasted in next time.
