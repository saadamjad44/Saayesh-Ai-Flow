## How to read these prices

Four things in the table change what you actually pay, and all four are easier to miss than the price itself.

**The billing unit is the most important column.** "Billed per" says whether a figure is a flat price for the whole account or a price per person. A $20 flat plan and a $20 seat look identical in a list and are completely different products once a second person needs access: one stays $20, the other becomes $40, then $60. Per-seat pricing is what turns a manageable AI budget into an unmanageable one, and it does it quietly.

**The annual column is a monthly equivalent, not a monthly charge.** Where a plan lists an annual price, we show it as the per-month figure you divide by — because that is what makes it comparable to the monthly column. What you are actually billed is twelve times that, up front. Several rows note the up-front amount for exactly this reason.

**A "from" price is a floor.** Usage-billed products, automation platforms especially, sell one plan name across several volume tiers at different prices. The figure we record is the lowest of those. If your month is busier than the smallest tier allows, you pay more, or you are pushed onto the next plan.

**The usage cap is part of the price.** A $20 plan with a monthly task, credit, prompt or minute allowance you will exceed is not a $20 plan. The "Limits and notes" column carries each cap in the vendor's own words, along with seat minimums, promotional rates that expire, and any arithmetic we did ourselves.

## Where these figures come from

Each row began life in one of our guides. Those guides state prices because a reader budgeting for AI needs real numbers, and each one records the date its prices were checked on the vendor's own pricing page.

The problem was that the data stayed trapped in prose. A figure in the middle of a 3,000-word article cannot be compared against one in a different article, written on a different date, in a different cluster of the site. So this page consolidates them under five rules:

1. **A row may only restate a figure a page on this site already publishes.** This page does no new research and makes no new claims.
2. **The check date is the date that page recorded,** never today's date and never a guess.
3. **Where two guides disagree, the newer check wins** and the older figure is removed rather than kept beside it.
4. **Any arithmetic of ours is stated on the row.** Where a vendor publishes only a yearly price, the monthly equivalent is our own division, and the row says so.
5. **Prices are US list prices excluding tax.** Vendors localise, discount and reprice constantly.

Every row links back to the guide that published it, so you can read the figure in its original context — with the caveats that guide attached to it.

## What this dataset is not

This matters more than what it is, because a table of numbers invites conclusions it cannot support.

**It is not a market average.** The tools are here because they appear in our guides, which is the only selection rule. That makes this a census of what we have checked, not a random sample of the industry, so the medians on this page describe this table and nothing wider. Anyone quoting them as an industry figure would be wrong.

**It is not a record of what anyone paid.** These are advertised list prices. Real invoices include tax, local currency, promotional rates, negotiated discounts, seat minimums you did not want, and usage you did not forecast.

**It is not a ranking.** There are no scores here and no best-value verdict, because judging value needs hands-on testing this site does not claim. Price is the one thing about these products that is published, checkable and comparable, so price is all this page offers.

**It is not a substitute for the vendor's pricing page.** It is a way of seeing the whole field at once and knowing how old each figure is. Before you spend money, click through.

## How often it is re-checked

Re-verification is a manual pass: open each vendor's pricing page, correct anything that moved, and set that row's date to the day we looked. There is no scraper, because a scraper reading a JavaScript-rendered pricing page reliably enough to publish from is a bigger engineering problem than it sounds, and a wrong price published confidently is worse than an old one published honestly.

So instead of promising freshness, the page measures it. Rows are marked for re-checking after a month and flagged after three, and the counts at the top tell you how much of the table is in each state right now. If a row you care about is flagged, treat that as what it is: a signal to check the vendor yourself.

## Use the data

The whole table is published as JSON at [/ai-tool-pricing.json](/ai-tool-pricing.json) under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence. Each row carries its check date, its source, and an absolute link to the page that published it, and the file leads with the licence, the currency and the limitations so they travel with the data.

Use it commercially, build on it, chart it. The one thing we ask is that you **keep the check date attached to the price**, because a list price without a date is not a fact — it is a rumour with a dollar sign on it.
