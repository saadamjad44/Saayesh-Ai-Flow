Most AI coverage is written for one of two audiences: people deciding whether to invest in the industry, or people building on top of it. Neither of them is you if you are running a business and simply want to know whether this week's announcement changes anything about the tools you already pay for.

That gap is what this section is for. The test for publishing a story here is not whether it is interesting — plenty of it is — but whether something practical follows from it.

## How these stories are written

Four rules, which are worth stating because a lot of AI reporting follows none of them.

- **Confirmed is separated from claimed.** A vendor's description of its own product is a claim until someone independent has checked it. Where that distinction matters, the page says which it is.
- **Retractions are reported as prominently as the original.** Fast stories routinely carry claims that the people who made them later withdraw. Someone finding a story a week after it broke needs to know that more than they need the original headline.
- **Numbers are attributed.** Figures in this subject vary wildly between sources, often by a factor of two, so a number here is given with whoever published it attached.
- **Every story lists its sources.** At the bottom of each one, linked, so you can read the primary material yourself instead of trusting a summary.

## What gets covered, and what does not

Covered: pricing changes, availability and regional restrictions, usage limits, outages, security problems in software you are likely to be running, and product launches that are actually usable rather than announced.

Not covered: funding rounds, benchmark scores, research papers, and executive moves — all of them covered better elsewhere, and none of them a reason to change what you do on Monday.
